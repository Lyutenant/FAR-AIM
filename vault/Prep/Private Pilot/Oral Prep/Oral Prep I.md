**Scenario 1.** You last flew 95 days ago, in daylight. A friend wants to go up with you at 2100 tonight. Legal?
**Answer (study aid):** No. To carry passengers you need three takeoffs and landings in the preceding 90 days, and for a night flight they must be to a full stop at night in the same category and class. Go fly three full-stop night landings alone first.
**Find it:** Part 61 subpart A, the 61.5x range: § 61.56 is the flight review, § 61.57 is recency.
**Cites:** [[61.57|§ 61.57 — Recent flight experience: Pilot in command]]

**Scenario 2.** Your flight review was 25 months ago but you have flown 100 hours since. May you act as PIC?
**Answer (study aid):** No. A flight review within the preceding 24 calendar months is required regardless of hours flown; a new certificate or rating, or a completed WINGS phase, would also satisfy it.
**Find it:** § 61.56 — the review is dated by calendar months, not by hours.
**Cites:** [[61.56|§ 61.56 — Flight review]]

**Scenario 3.** You are 40 and got a third-class medical 4 years ago. Can you fly as a private pilot today?
**Answer (study aid):** Yes, until the end of the 60th calendar month after the exam, because you were under 40 on the exam date; at 40 or older it would have been 24 calendar months. Check the date on the certificate and the § 61.23 table.
**Find it:** § 61.23(d), the duration table, keyed to age on the date of the examination.
**Cites:** [[61.23|§ 61.23 — Medical certificates: Requirement and duration]], [[61.3|§ 61.3 — Requirement for certificates, ratings, privileges, and authorizations]]

**Scenario 4.** A ramp inspector asks for your documents at the fuel pump. What do you show?
**Answer (study aid):** Pilot certificate, government photo ID and medical certificate (or the BasicMed items), and the logbook only if you are a student on a solo cross-country or if you carry it; § 61.51(i) requires presenting the logbook on reasonable request when you have it.
**Find it:** § 61.3 for what you carry, § 61.51(i) for what you must present.
**Cites:** [[61.3|§ 61.3 — Requirement for certificates, ratings, privileges, and authorizations]], [[61.51|§ 61.51 — Pilot logbooks]]

**Scenario 5.** Your coworker offers to pay for the whole flight if you fly them to a meeting. Can you accept as a private pilot?
**Answer (study aid):** No. A private pilot may not act as PIC for compensation or hire; you may share expenses only pro rata, paying at least your own share, and only for fuel, oil, airport expenses and rental. Providing transportation for someone else's business purpose is the classic holding-out trap.
**Find it:** § 61.113: privileges first, then the exceptions (b) through (i).
**Cites:** [[61.113|§ 61.113 — Private pilot privileges and limitations: Pilot in command]]

**Scenario 6.** You want to fly under BasicMed. What do you need and what can you not do?
**Answer (study aid):** A U.S. driver's license, a medical held after July 14, 2006, the physician exam on the FAA checklist every 48 months, the online course every 24 calendar months, and its certificate in your logbook. Limits: aircraft up to 6 passengers and 12,500 lb, below 18,000 ft and 250 knots, not for compensation or hire, in the United States.
**Find it:** § 61.113(i) for the limits, Part 68 for the course and checklist.
**Cites:** [[61.113|§ 61.113 — Private pilot privileges and limitations: Pilot in command]], [[68.3|§ 68.3 — Medical education course requirements]], [[61.23|§ 61.23 — Medical certificates: Requirement and duration]]

**Scenario 7.** You moved last month and forgot to tell the FAA. Any problem?
**Answer (study aid):** You may not exercise your certificate privileges after 30 days from the move until you notify the FAA Airman Certification Branch of the new address.
**Find it:** § 61.60, change of address.
**Cites:** [[61.60|§ 61.60 — Change of address]]

**Scenario 8.** What does the examiner mean when asking about proficiency versus currency?
**Answer (study aid):** Currency is the legal minimum: flight review, three landings in 90 days, night landings. Proficiency is being able to do it well today. You can be current and unsafe; set personal minimums above the regulation.
**Cites:** [[61.57|§ 61.57 — Recent flight experience: Pilot in command]], [[61.56|§ 61.56 — Flight review]], [[8-1-1|AIM 8-1-1 — Fitness For Flight]]

**Scenario 1.** Show me how you know this airplane is airworthy today.
**Answer (study aid):** Airworthiness certificate displayed and registration aboard, operating limitations (POH, placards) available; maintenance records showing the annual within 12 calendar months, the 100-hour if it is used for hire, transponder within 24 calendar months, ELT battery, and airworthiness directive compliance; then my own preflight under § 91.7 says it is in condition for safe flight.
**Find it:** Part 91 subpart E (91.4xx) for inspections and records; § 91.203 for documents.
**Cites:** [[91.203|§ 91.203 — Civil aircraft: Certifications required]], [[91.9|§ 91.9 — Civil aircraft flight manual, marking, and placard requirements]], [[91.409|§ 91.409 — Inspections]], [[91.413|§ 91.413 — ATC transponder tests and inspections]], [[91.207|§ 91.207 — Emergency locator transmitters]], [[91.417|§ 91.417 — Maintenance records]], [[91.7|§ 91.7 — Civil aircraft airworthiness]]

**Scenario 2.** The annual was signed 13 months ago but the airplane has flown only 20 hours. May you fly it?
**Answer (study aid):** No. The annual is due every 12 calendar months regardless of hours. It may be flown only under a special flight permit to a place where the inspection can be done.
**Find it:** § 91.409(a); the ferry permit is § 21.197.
**Cites:** [[91.409|§ 91.409 — Inspections]], [[21.197|§ 21.197 — Special flight permits]]

**Scenario 3.** The turn coordinator is inoperative on a day VFR flight in a Cessna 172. Can you go?
**Answer (study aid):** Possibly, using § 91.213(d): it is not required by § 91.205(b) for day VFR, not on the type certificate's required list, not required by an AD, and not on a minimum equipment list. Deactivate or remove it, placard it inoperative, and as PIC decide it is safe. Then it must be repaired, replaced or inspected at the next required inspection.
**Find it:** § 91.213(d) is the four-question test; § 91.205(b) is the day VFR list.
**Cites:** [[91.213|§ 91.213 — Inoperative instruments and equipment]], [[91.205|§ 91.205 — Powered civil aircraft with standard U.S. airworthiness certificates: Instrument and equipment requirements]], [[91.405|§ 91.405 — Maintenance required]]

**Scenario 4.** May you change the oil and replace the tires on the club airplane you fly?
**Answer (study aid):** If you are a pilot who owns or operates the airplane and it is not used under Part 121, 129 or 135, yes: both are preventive maintenance in Part 43 Appendix A, and you must make the § 43.9 logbook entry with the date, description, your signature and certificate number.
**Find it:** § 43.3(g) grants it; § 43.9 records it; Appendix A lists it.
**Cites:** [[43.3|§ 43.3 — Persons authorized to perform maintenance, preventive maintenance, rebuilding, and alterations]], [[43.9|§ 43.9 — Content, form, and disposition of maintenance, preventive maintenance, rebuilding, and alteration records (except inspections performed in accordance with part 91, part 125, § 135.411(a)(1), and § 135.419 of this chapter)]]

**Scenario 5.** Who is responsible for complying with an airworthiness directive, and how would you check?
**Answer (study aid):** The owner or operator under § 91.403; I check the AD compliance list the maintenance records must contain, with each AD's method of compliance and the next due date for recurring ADs.
**Cites:** [[91.403|§ 91.403 — General]], [[91.417|§ 91.417 — Maintenance records]]

**Scenario 6.** What must be in the airplane for a night VFR flight beyond the day VFR list?
**Answer (study aid):** Approved position lights, an approved anticollision light system, an adequate source of electrical energy for all installed electrical and radio equipment, one spare set of fuses (or three of each kind), and if operated for hire, a landing light.
**Find it:** § 91.205(c); § 91.209 says when to use the lights.
**Cites:** [[91.205|§ 91.205 — Powered civil aircraft with standard U.S. airworthiness certificates: Instrument and equipment requirements]], [[91.209|§ 91.209 — Aircraft lights]]

**Scenario 1.** Where did you get your weather this morning and does that count as a legal briefing?
**Answer (study aid):** A self-briefing from the aviationweather.gov Graphical Forecasts for Aviation, METARs, TAFs, winds aloft and NOTAMs, following AC 91-92; the AIM says a pilot can receive a regulatory-compliant briefing without contacting Flight Service. § 91.103 requires familiarity with all available information.
**Find it:** AIM 5-1-1 and 7-1-5 for briefings; § 91.103 for the legal duty.
**Cites:** [[5-1-1|AIM 5-1-1 — Preflight Preparation]], [[91.103|§ 91.103 — Preflight action]], [[7-1-4|AIM 7-1-4 — Graphical Forecasts for Aviation (GFA)]]

**Scenario 2.** The briefer says 'VFR flight not recommended'. Are you grounded?
**Answer (study aid):** No, it is advisory. It means the briefer judges VFR doubtful along the route; the go decision is mine, but I need a specific reason and an out before overriding it.
**Cites:** [[7-1-5|AIM 7-1-5 — Preflight Briefing]]

**Scenario 3.** Decode this METAR: KPIT 091955Z 22015G25KT 3/4SM R28L/2600FT TSRA OVC010CB 18/16 A2992.
**Answer (study aid):** Pittsburgh, 9th at 1955Z, wind 220 true at 15 gusting 25, visibility 3/4 statute mile, RVR runway 28L 2,600 ft, thunderstorm with rain, overcast 1,000 ft cumulonimbus, temperature 18 dew point 16, altimeter 29.92. Not VFR.
**Cites:** [[7-1-28|AIM 7-1-28 — Key to Aerodrome Forecast (TAF) and Aviation Routine Weather Report (METAR)]]

**Scenario 4.** An AIRMET Sierra covers your destination. What is it and what do you do?
**Answer (study aid):** IFR conditions or mountain obscuration expected over a wide area for a VFR pilot's purposes; check the TAF and current METARs for the destination and alternates and plan to divert or delay if the AIRMET verifies.
**Find it:** AIM 7-1-6: SIGMET, convective SIGMET, AIRMET, CWA.
**Cites:** [[7-1-6|AIM 7-1-6 — Inflight Aviation Weather Advisories]]

**Scenario 5.** A convective SIGMET is issued for your route. What does it imply even if it does not say so?
**Answer (study aid):** Severe or greater turbulence, severe icing and low-level wind shear. Stay at least 20 miles from severe storms and circumnavigate an area with 6/10 coverage.
**Cites:** [[7-1-6|AIM 7-1-6 — Inflight Aviation Weather Advisories]], [[7-1-27|AIM 7-1-27 — Thunderstorm Flying]], [[7-1-26|AIM 7-1-26 — Thunderstorms]]

**Scenario 6.** Your tablet shows NEXRAD from FIS-B with a gap between two cells. Can you thread it?
**Answer (study aid):** No. FIS-B radar is minutes old, the AIM says it is not to be used for tactical avoidance, and thunderstorm hazards extend well outside the echoes. Use it strategically to go around the whole area.
**Cites:** [[4-5-9|AIM 4-5-9 — Flight Information Service- Broadcast (FIS-B)]], [[7-1-9|AIM 7-1-9 — Flight Information Services (FIS)]], [[7-1-27|AIM 7-1-27 — Thunderstorm Flying]]

**Scenario 7.** The temperature and dew point at your destination are 12 and 11 with a forecast of calm winds after sunset. Concern?
**Answer (study aid):** A small spread and cooling favor fog or low clouds after dark; plan to arrive well before, check the TAF for BR or FG, and have an alternate with a wider spread.
**Cites:** [[7-1-28|AIM 7-1-28 — Key to Aerodrome Forecast (TAF) and Aviation Routine Weather Report (METAR)]], [[7-1-5|AIM 7-1-5 — Preflight Briefing]]

**Scenario 1.** Plan the fuel for a 2-hour day VFR flight. What is the legal minimum and what is your own?
**Answer (study aid):** Legally, enough to reach the first point of intended landing plus 30 minutes at normal cruise by day (45 at night). Personally I plan to land with an hour, and I compute it from the POH cruise figures plus taxi, climb and reserve.
**Find it:** § 91.151 for VFR; § 91.167 is the IFR rule.
**Cites:** [[91.151|§ 91.151 — Fuel requirements for flight in VFR conditions]]

**Scenario 2.** Your course is 095 magnetic and you want to cruise at 6,500 ft. Legal?
**Answer (study aid):** Not above 3,000 ft AGL: an eastbound course (0 to 179) takes odd thousands plus 500, so 5,500 or 7,500. Below 3,000 ft AGL the rule does not apply.
**Cites:** [[91.159|§ 91.159 — VFR cruising altitude or flight level]], [[3-1-5|AIM 3-1-5 — VFR Cruising Altitudes and Flight Levels]]

**Scenario 3.** What goes on a VFR flight plan and how do you activate and close it?
**Answer (study aid):** Aircraft identification and type, PIC name and address, departure point and time, route, altitude and true airspeed, first intended landing point and ETE, fuel in hours, souls on board. File with Flight Service or an app, activate by radio or electronically after takeoff (the tower will not), and close it on landing; search begins 30 minutes after the ETA.
**Find it:** § 91.153 lists the contents; AIM 5-1-5 and 5-1-14 the procedures.
**Cites:** [[91.153|§ 91.153 — VFR flight plan: Information required]], [[5-1-5|AIM 5-1-5 — Flight Plan - VFR Flights]], [[5-1-14|AIM 5-1-14 — Closing VFR/DVFR Flight Plans]]

**Scenario 4.** You are intercepted by a fighter near a TFR. What do you do?
**Answer (study aid):** Rock the wings and follow, call on 121.5 with identity, position and nature of flight, squawk 7700 unless told otherwise, and comply with every signal until released. Circling the runway with gear down means land here.
**Cites:** [[5-6-13|AIM 5-6-13 — Interception Procedures]], [[5-6-14|AIM 5-6-14 — Interception Signals]]

**Scenario 5.** You are on flight following. Does that keep you out of restricted areas and Class B?
**Answer (study aid):** No. Traffic advisories are workload permitting and the pilot remains responsible for airspace: the AIM says the pilot must meet clearance and communication requirements before entering Class B, C or D, and permission for restricted airspace comes from the controlling agency.
**Cites:** [[3-2-1|AIM 3-2-1 — General]], [[4-1-15|AIM 4-1-15 — Radar Traffic Information Service]], [[91.133|§ 91.133 — Restricted and prohibited areas]]

**Scenario 6.** Passengers are waiting and the ceiling at the destination is dropping. Walk me through PAVE.
**Answer (study aid):** Pilot (IMSAFE, currency), Aircraft (fuel, equipment), enVironment (weather trend, terrain, airspace, alternates, daylight) and External pressures (the waiting passengers). The pressure is the hazard; the mitigation is a decision made on the ground with a divert point and an alternate.
**Cites:** [[8-1-1|AIM 8-1-1 — Fitness For Flight]], [[7-1-5|AIM 7-1-5 — Preflight Briefing]], [[91.103|§ 91.103 — Preflight action]]

**Scenario 1.** You are at 2,500 ft MSL, 20 miles from a Class C airport whose shelf begins at 4,000 ft. Do you need to talk to anyone?
**Answer (study aid):** Not for the Class C, because you are below its shelf, but you are inside the Mode C veil only if it is a Class B airport; for Class C the transponder and ADS-B rule applies within and above the Class C area itself. Check whether the underlying airspace is Class E or a Class D surface area.
**Find it:** § 91.130 for Class C; § 91.215(b) and § 91.225(d) for the equipment map.
**Cites:** [[91.130|§ 91.130 — Operations in Class C airspace]], [[91.215|§ 91.215 — ATC transponder and altitude reporting equipment and use]], [[91.225|§ 91.225 — Automatic Dependent Surveillance-Broadcast (ADS-B) Out equipment and use]]

**Scenario 2.** What are the VFR minimums in Class G at 700 ft AGL at night, and in Class E at 9,500 ft?
**Answer (study aid):** Class G below 1,200 ft AGL at night: 3 statute miles, 500 below, 1,000 above, 2,000 horizontal (unless in the pattern under the § 91.155(b) exception). Class E below 10,000 ft: 3 miles and 500/1,000/2,000.
**Cites:** [[91.155|§ 91.155 — Basic VFR weather minimums]], [[3-1-4|AIM 3-1-4 — Basic VFR Weather Minimums]]

**Scenario 3.** The tower at a Class D airport is closed for the night. What airspace is it now and what are the rules?
**Answer (study aid):** Usually Class E or G as the chart supplement states; without the tower the communication requirement of § 91.129 no longer applies, you self-announce on the CTAF, and left traffic applies unless right traffic is indicated.
**Cites:** [[91.129|§ 91.129 — Operations in Class D airspace]], [[91.127|§ 91.127 — Operating on or in the vicinity of an airport in Class E airspace]], [[91.126|§ 91.126 — Operating on or in the vicinity of an airport in Class G airspace]], [[4-1-9|AIM 4-1-9 — Traffic Advisory Practices at Airports Without Operating Control Towers]]

**Scenario 4.** Approach says 'radar contact, remain clear of the Class Bravo'. What may you do?
**Answer (study aid):** Exactly that: you are identified but not cleared. Entry requires the words 'cleared into the Class Bravo airspace' from ATC; stay outside until you hear them.
**Cites:** [[91.131|§ 91.131 — Operations in Class B airspace]], [[3-2-1|AIM 3-2-1 — General]]

**Scenario 5.** Is it legal to fly through a MOA that is active? A restricted area? A prohibited area?
**Answer (study aid):** MOA: yes, with extreme caution and a call to the controlling agency for advisories. Restricted: only when inactive or with permission of the using or controlling agency. Prohibited: no.
**Cites:** [[3-4-5|AIM 3-4-5 — Military Operations Areas]], [[3-4-3|AIM 3-4-3 — Restricted Areas]], [[3-4-2|AIM 3-4-2 — Prohibited Areas]], [[91.133|§ 91.133 — Restricted and prohibited areas]]

**Scenario 6.** The ceiling is 900 ft overcast, visibility 4 miles, at your Class D home field. Can you depart?
**Answer (study aid):** Only with a special VFR clearance from the tower: at least 1 statute mile ground visibility, clear of clouds, day only unless instrument rated in an IFR airplane, and you must request it.
**Cites:** [[91.157|§ 91.157 — Special VFR weather minimums]], [[4-4-6|AIM 4-4-6 — Special VFR Clearances]]

**Scenario 7.** Where is ADS-B Out required and where is it not?
**Answer (study aid):** Class A, B and C, within the 30 nm Mode C veil of the Appendix D airports, above Class B or C up to 10,000 ft, Class E at and above 10,000 ft MSL except within 2,500 ft of the surface, and over the Gulf above 3,000 ft. Not in Class D, Class G, or low Class E outside a veil.
**Cites:** [[91.225|§ 91.225 — Automatic Dependent Surveillance-Broadcast (ADS-B) Out equipment and use]], [[91.215|§ 91.215 — ATC transponder and altitude reporting equipment and use]]

**Scenario 1.** Field elevation 5,000 ft, temperature 30 C. Why does the airplane take longer to get off and climb worse?
**Answer (study aid):** High density altitude: the POH figures assume 15 C and 29.92 at sea level; hot and high air is thin, so the engine makes less power, the propeller and wings get less air, and the same indicated speeds need a higher true airspeed and a longer ground roll. Airports at 2,000 ft or higher broadcast a density altitude advisory for this reason.
**Find it:** AIM 7-6-7 for density altitude; the numbers come from the POH performance section.
**Cites:** [[7-6-7|AIM 7-6-7 — Mountain Flying]]

**Scenario 2.** Where are the airspeed limitations and the weight and balance limits, and are they regulatory?
**Answer (study aid):** In the approved flight manual, the placards and the markings; § 91.9 makes operating within them mandatory and requires the manual to be aboard.
**Cites:** [[91.9|§ 91.9 — Civil aircraft flight manual, marking, and placard requirements]]

**Scenario 3.** You compute a 1,800 ft ground roll but the runway is 2,000 ft with a displaced threshold. Comfortable?
**Answer (study aid):** No. The chart assumes a perfect technique in a new airplane; add a margin (a common rule is 50 percent), and check the declared distances and the displaced threshold in the Chart Supplement, because the landing distance available is not the pavement length.
**Cites:** [[4-3-6|AIM 4-3-6 — Use of Runways/Declared Distances]], [[2-3-3|AIM 2-3-3 — Runway Markings]]

**Scenario 1.** In cruise the oil pressure needle drops toward the red line with normal oil temperature. What do you do?
**Answer (study aid):** Treat it as an impending engine failure: reduce power, turn toward the nearest suitable airport, run the checklist, and if temperature starts rising too, plan a precautionary landing. The POH emergency section is the authority; declare with ATC if in doubt.
**Cites:** [[6-1-2|AIM 6-1-2 — Emergency Condition- Request Assistance Immediately]], [[6-3-2|AIM 6-3-2 — Obtaining Emergency Assistance]]

**Scenario 2.** Your alternator light comes on. What fails next and what do you protect?
**Answer (study aid):** The battery now carries the electrical load; shed non-essential loads, expect the radios, transponder and flaps (if electric) to die when it depletes, and land at a towered field only if you can still talk; otherwise be ready for light signals.
**Cites:** [[4-2-13|AIM 4-2-13 — Communications with Tower when Aircraft Transmitter or Receiver or Both are Inoperative]], [[4-3-13|AIM 4-3-13 — Traffic Control Light Signals]], [[6-4-2|AIM 6-4-2 — Transponder Operation During Two‐way Communications Failure]]

**Scenario 3.** You smell exhaust with the cabin heat on and feel a headache. What is happening?
**Answer (study aid):** Probable carbon monoxide from a cracked exhaust or heater shroud: heater off, vents open, land as soon as practical and seek medical attention if symptoms persist.
**Cites:** [[8-1-4|AIM 8-1-4 — Carbon Monoxide Poisoning in Flight]]

**Scenario 1.** You had two beers at 2100 last night and it is 0700. Legal to fly at 0800?
**Answer (study aid):** Eight hours have passed, so the 8-hour rule is met, but you must also not be under the influence and must be below 0.04 percent; the AIM warns you may still be impaired after 8 hours and a hangover counts. If in doubt, no.
**Find it:** § 91.17 is the rule; AIM 8-1-1 is the physiology.
**Cites:** [[91.17|§ 91.17 — Alcohol or drugs]], [[8-1-1|AIM 8-1-1 — Fitness For Flight]]

**Scenario 2.** You have a head cold but feel fine on the ground. Go?
**Answer (study aid):** No. Congestion blocks the eustachian tubes and sinuses; on descent an ear or sinus block can be excruciating and damaging, and decongestants have side effects. § 61.53 also prohibits flying with a condition that would make you unable to meet the medical standards.
**Cites:** [[8-1-2|AIM 8-1-2 — Effects of Altitude]], [[61.53|§ 61.53 — Prohibition on operations during medical deficiency]]

**Scenario 3.** At 11,500 ft at night you feel euphoric and your fingertips tingle. What is it and what do you do?
**Answer (study aid):** Hypoxia (night vision degrades from 5,000 ft and other effects begin around 12,000), possibly compounded by hyperventilation. Go on oxygen if you have it and descend; below 10,000 by day and 5,000 at night is the AIM's recommendation.
**Cites:** [[8-1-2|AIM 8-1-2 — Effects of Altitude]], [[8-1-3|AIM 8-1-3 — Hyperventilation in Flight]], [[91.211|§ 91.211 — Supplemental oxygen]]

**Scenario 4.** Name the five hazardous attitudes and their antidotes.
**Answer (study aid):** Anti-authority (follow the rules, they are usually right), impulsivity (not so fast, think first), invulnerability (it could happen to me), macho (taking chances is foolish), resignation (I am not helpless, I can make a difference). Source: the Pilot's Handbook chapter on ADM, not the FAR.
**Cites:** [[8-1-1|AIM 8-1-1 — Fitness For Flight]]

**Scenario 5.** You went scuba diving this morning to 40 ft with no decompression stop. When can you fly at 6,500 ft?
**Answer (study aid):** Wait at least 12 hours; 24 hours if the dive needed a decompression stop or if you will fly above 8,000 ft.
**Cites:** [[8-1-2|AIM 8-1-2 — Effects of Altitude]]
