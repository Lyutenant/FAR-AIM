## Subpart A — General

§ 91.1 – § 91.27-91.99 (14 sections) · **6 studied**

- [[91.3|§ 91.3]] — The pilot in command is directly responsible for, and the final authority as to, the operation of the aircraft; in an in-flight emergency requiring immediate action the PIC may deviate from any Part 91 rule to the extent the emergency requires, and must send a written report only if the Administrator asks for one.
- [[91.7|§ 91.7]] — Two sentences that carry a lot of weight: no one may operate a civil aircraft unless it is airworthy, and the PIC is responsible for deciding whether it is in condition for safe flight and must discontinue the flight when an unairworthy condition occurs.
- [[91.9|§ 91.9]] — Fly within the operating limitations in the approved flight manual, markings and placards, and carry a current approved flight manual (or the approved manual material, markings and placards for older aircraft) in the aircraft. The aircraft must also be identified under Part 45.
- [[91.17|§ 91.17]] — No crewmember within 8 hours of drinking, while under the influence, with an alcohol concentration of 0.04 or greater, or while using any drug that affects safety; and, except in an emergency, no carrying a passenger who appears intoxicated.
- [[91.19|§ 91.19]] — No one may operate a civil aircraft in the United States knowing that narcotic drugs, marijuana or depressant or stimulant drugs are on board, unless the carriage is authorized by law.
- [[91.21|§ 91.21]] — Portable electronic devices may not be operated on air-carrier aircraft or on any aircraft under IFR, except voice recorders, hearing aids, pacemakers, electric shavers, and any device the operator has determined will not interfere with navigation or communication. For other aircraft that determination can be made by the PIC.

## Subpart B — Flight Rules

§ 91.101 – § 91.195-91.199 (55 sections) · **25 studied**

- [[91.103|§ 91.103]] — Before every flight the PIC must become familiar with all available information: for flights away from the airport or under IFR, weather reports and forecasts, fuel requirements, alternatives and any known ATC delays; for every flight, runway lengths and takeoff and landing distance data.
- [[91.105|§ 91.105]] — Required flight crewmembers stay at their station during takeoff, landing and en route with the safety belt fastened, and keep the shoulder harness fastened for takeoff and landing if the seat has one and it does not prevent them doing their duties.
- [[91.107|§ 91.107]] — The PIC must brief everyone on how to fasten and unfasten their safety belt and shoulder harness, notify them to fasten before surface movement, takeoff and landing, and everyone must occupy an approved seat with the belt fastened at those times; a child under two may be held.
- [[91.113|§ 91.113]] — See and avoid always applies. Converging: the aircraft on the right has the right of way, but balloons, gliders, airships and aircraft towing or refueling rank above powered aircraft; head-on, both alter course right; the overtaken aircraft has the right of way; aircraft landing or on final have it over others, and the lower of two approaching aircraft — but neither may abuse it.
- [[91.115|§ 91.115]] — Right-of-way on the water: keep clear of vessels and do not impede them. On crossing courses the craft to the other's right has the right-of-way, head-on both alter course to the right, and the craft being overtaken has the right-of-way.
- [[91.117|§ 91.117]] — Speed limits: 250 knots indicated below 10,000 ft MSL; 200 knots at or below 2,500 ft AGL within 4 NM of the primary airport of a Class C or D area; 200 knots beneath a Class B area or in a VFR corridor through it.
- [[91.119|§ 91.119]] — Minimum safe altitudes: always high enough to make an emergency landing without undue hazard if the engine fails; over congested areas 1,000 ft above the highest obstacle within a 2,000 ft radius; elsewhere 500 ft above the surface, or over open water and sparsely populated areas no closer than 500 ft to any person, vessel, vehicle or structure.
- [[91.121|§ 91.121]] — What to set in the altimeter: below 18,000 ft MSL, the current reported setting of a station along the route within 100 nm, or an appropriate available station, or without a radio the departure airport elevation; at or above 18,000 ft MSL, 29.92. The tables give the lowest usable flight level for low pressure.
- [[91.123|§ 91.123]] — Once you have a clearance you may not deviate from it unless you get an amended clearance, an emergency exists, or you are responding to a TCAS resolution advisory. Except in an emergency, you may not operate contrary to an ATC instruction where ATC is exercised. If you deviate you must notify ATC as soon as possible, and if ATC gave you priority in an emergency you must file a detailed report within 48 hours if asked. Never act on a clearance issued to another aircraft.
- [[91.125|§ 91.125]] — The light-gun signals are the regulation, not just an AIM table. The section is a single sentence pointing to its table: steady green, flashing green, steady red, flashing red, flashing white, and alternating red and green each have a fixed meaning on the ground and in flight.
- [[91.126|§ 91.126]] — Operating at an airport in Class G airspace: approaching to land, powered fixed-wing aircraft make all turns to the left unless the airport shows light signals or markings for right traffic, and other aircraft avoid that flow. If the airport has an operating tower, keep two-way radio communications with it, established before 4 nm out and up to 2,500 ft AGL.
- [[91.127|§ 91.127]] — Operating at an airport in Class E airspace: follow the § 91.126 rules (left traffic unless indicated) and any Part 93 traffic pattern, and if the airport has an operating tower keep two-way radio communications from 4 nm and 2,500 ft AGL inward.
- [[91.129|§ 91.129]] — Class D: establish two-way radio communications with the tower before entering and maintain them inside; a clearance is required to taxi, take off or land at a towered airport; if the radio fails in flight under VFR you may land with basic VFR weather, visual contact with the tower and a light-signal clearance.
- [[91.130|§ 91.130]] — Class C adds to the Class D rules: two-way radio communications must be established with the facility providing ATC services before entry and maintained inside, and the aircraft must have a Mode C transponder and ADS-B Out.
- [[91.131|§ 91.131]] — Class B: an ATC clearance is required before entry; the PIC must hold at least a private pilot certificate (or be a student, recreational or sport pilot with the required training and endorsement, except at the airports listed in appendix D section 4); the aircraft needs a two-way radio, a Mode C transponder and ADS-B Out.
- [[91.133|§ 91.133]] — You may not fly in a restricted area contrary to its restrictions, or in a prohibited area at all, without the permission of the using or controlling agency.
- [[91.135|§ 91.135]] — Class A airspace (18,000 ft MSL to FL600) is IFR only: an ATC clearance before entry, two-way radio with ATC, and a transponder and ADS-B Out. Deviations are possible only by ATC authorization.
- [[91.137|§ 91.137]] — Temporary flight restrictions for disasters and hazards. A NOTAM designates the area for one of three reasons: protect people from a surface hazard, provide a safe environment for relief aircraft, or prevent sightseeing congestion over an event. Who may enter depends on which paragraph the NOTAM cites; for a sightseeing TFR, flights to or from an airport inside the area and IFR flights may continue.
- [[91.141|§ 91.141]] — No flight over or near an area the President, Vice President or other public figures are visiting, contrary to the restrictions the FAA publishes by NOTAM.
- [[91.145|§ 91.145]] — TFRs for aerial demonstrations and major sporting events (airshows, the Super Bowl, the Indy 500 and similar). The FAA publishes a NOTAM at least 30 days ahead when it can; the restricted airspace is normally limited to a 3 nm radius for sporting events, and no one may operate inside it except as the NOTAM allows or with ATC or waiver authorization.
- [[91.151|§ 91.151]] — Day VFR: enough fuel to fly to the first point of intended landing and then for at least 30 minutes at normal cruise; at night, at least 45 minutes.
- [[91.153|§ 91.153]] — What a VFR flight plan must contain: aircraft identification, type, PIC name and address, departure point and time, route, cruising altitude and true airspeed, first intended landing point and ETE, fuel on board in hours, persons aboard, and anything else ATC needs. Once activated, you must notify an FSS or ATC when you cancel or complete the flight.
- [[91.155|§ 91.155]] — Basic VFR weather minimums by airspace: 3 statute miles and 500 ft below, 1,000 ft above, 2,000 ft horizontal from clouds in Classes C, D and E below 10,000 ft; Class B is 3 miles clear of clouds; Class G by day below 1,200 ft AGL is 1 mile clear of clouds; at or above 10,000 ft MSL it is 5 miles and 1,000 below, 1,000 above, 1 mile horizontal. Plus: no VFR beneath a ceiling below 1,000 ft in a surface area, and 3 miles ground visibility to take off, land or enter the pattern there.
- [[91.157|§ 91.157]] — Special VFR lets you operate in a surface area below basic VFR minimums with an ATC clearance, clear of clouds, with at least 1 statute mile flight visibility (and 1 mile ground visibility to take off or land); between sunrise and sunset unless you are instrument rated and the aircraft is equipped for IFR.
- [[91.159|§ 91.159]] — VFR cruising altitudes apply in level flight more than 3,000 ft above the surface: on a magnetic course of 0° through 179° fly an odd thousand-foot MSL altitude plus 500 ft; on 180° through 359° fly an even thousand plus 500 ft.

## Subpart C — Equipment, Instrument, and Certificate Requirements

§ 91.201 – § 91.228-91.299 (16 sections) · **8 studied**

- [[91.203|§ 91.203]] — The paperwork that must be in the airplane: an appropriate and current airworthiness certificate, displayed at the cabin or cockpit entrance where it is legible, and an effective U.S. registration certificate (or the temporary alternatives the section lists). A fuel tank in the cabin needs a Form 337 aboard.
- [[91.205|§ 91.205]] — The instruments and equipment that must be installed and operable: paragraph (b) for day VFR (airspeed, altimeter, compass, tachometer, oil pressure and temperature gauges, fuel gauges, gear indicator, belts and harnesses, ELT and more), paragraph (c) adds for night VFR position lights, an anticollision light system, an adequate electrical source, spare fuses and a landing light if for hire; paragraph (d) is the IFR list. Anything inoperative goes through § 91.213.
- [[91.207|§ 91.207]] — Most U.S. airplanes must carry an operable automatic ELT; batteries are replaced after more than 1 cumulative hour of use or at 50 percent of their useful life, the ELT is inspected within 12 calendar months, and exemptions include training flights within 50 NM of the departure airport and up to 90 days with the ELT removed for repair.
- [[91.209|§ 91.209]] — From sunset to sunrise an aircraft must show lighted position lights, and an aircraft equipped with anticollision lights must have them on at all times unless the PIC decides safety requires them off.
- [[91.211|§ 91.211]] — Supplemental oxygen by cabin pressure altitude: the required crew must use it above 12,500 ft MSL for any part of the flight over 30 minutes, at all times above 14,000 ft, and every occupant must be provided with it above 15,000 ft.
- [[91.213|§ 91.213]] — Flying with inoperative equipment: without an MEL, a small non-turbine airplane may fly if the item is not required by the day-VFR type certification list, the aircraft's equipment list or KOEL, § 91.205 or any other rule for that kind of flight, or an AD; it is removed, or deactivated and placarded “Inoperative”, with maintenance recorded; and a pilot or mechanic determines it is no hazard.
- [[91.215|§ 91.215]] — A Mode C transponder is required in Class A, B and C airspace, within the 30 NM Mode C veil of Class B primary airports from the surface to 10,000 ft, above Class B and C to 10,000 ft, and at and above 10,000 ft MSL except within 2,500 ft of the surface; when installed and operable it must be on and replying in all controlled airspace. ADS-B Out (§ 91.225) covers the same footprint.
- [[91.225|§ 91.225]] — Where ADS-B Out is required: Class A; Class B and C; within 30 nm of the Appendix D airports (the Mode C veil) from the surface to 10,000 ft MSL; above the ceiling of Class B or C up to 10,000 ft; Class E at and above 10,000 ft MSL except at or below 2,500 ft AGL; and over the Gulf at and above 3,000 ft MSL out to 12 nm. Aircraft without an engine-driven electrical system are exempt from some of it. Equipped aircraft transmit at all times; an unequipped aircraft must request an ATC deviation at least an hour ahead.

## Subpart E — Maintenance, Preventive Maintenance, and Alterations

§ 91.401 – § 91.423-91.499 (13 sections) · **7 studied**

- [[91.403|§ 91.403]] — The owner or operator is primarily responsible for keeping the aircraft airworthy, including compliance with airworthiness directives (Part 39), and maintenance may only be done as Part 43 and this subpart prescribe.
- [[91.405|§ 91.405]] — Owner and operator duties: have the required inspections done and discrepancies repaired between them, make sure maintenance personnel record the return to service, have inoperative equipment permitted by § 91.213(d) repaired, replaced, removed or inspected at the next required inspection, and make sure inoperative instruments and equipment are placarded.
- [[91.407|§ 91.407]] — After maintenance the aircraft may not be operated until it is approved for return to service by an authorized person and the record entry is made. If the work may have appreciably changed the flight characteristics, no one other than crew may be carried until an appropriately rated pilot with at least a private pilot certificate flies it and logs a satisfactory operational check, unless ground tests prove the check unnecessary.
- [[91.409|§ 91.409]] — An annual inspection within the preceding 12 calendar months is required before any operation; an aircraft carrying persons for hire, or used to give flight instruction for hire, also needs a 100-hour inspection, which may be exceeded by up to 10 hours to reach a place where it can be done.
- [[91.411|§ 91.411]] — For IFR in controlled airspace, the static system, each altimeter and the automatic altitude-reporting system must have been tested and inspected within the preceding 24 calendar months, and again after the static system is opened or the encoder worked on.
- [[91.413|§ 91.413]] — Any ATC transponder you use must have been tested and inspected within the preceding 24 calendar months, and re-tested after any installation or maintenance that could introduce a data error. Only a rated repair station, a continuous-airworthiness program holder or the manufacturer may do it.
- [[91.417|§ 91.417]] — Maintenance records the owner or operator must keep: records of maintenance, preventive maintenance, alterations and inspections (description, date, signature and certificate number), plus the running status records (total time, life-limited parts, overhaul times, inspection status, AD status with method of compliance, and Form 337s). The first set is kept for a year or until the work is repeated; the status records stay with the aircraft when sold. Records must be available to the FAA and NTSB.
