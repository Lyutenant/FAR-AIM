If the airport has an operating tower, keep two-way radio communications with it, established before 4 nm out and up to 2,500 ft AGL. |
| [[91.127|§ 91.127 — Operating on or in the vicinity of an airport in Class E airspace]] | Operating at an airport in Class E airspace: follow the § 91.126 rules (left traffic unless indicated) and any Part 93 traffic pattern, and if the airport has an operating tower keep two-way radio communications from 4 nm and 2,500 ft AGL inward. |
| [[91.129|§ 91.129 — Operations in Class D airspace]] | Class D: establish two-way radio communications with the tower before entering and maintain them inside; a clearance is required to taxi, take off or land at a towered airport; if the radio fails in flight under VFR you may land with basic VFR weather, visual contact with the tower and a light-signal clearance. |
| [[91.130|§ 91.130 — Operations in Class C airspace]] | Class C adds to the Class D rules: two-way radio communications must be established with the facility providing ATC services before entry and maintained inside, and the aircraft must have a Mode C transponder and ADS-B Out. |
| [[91.131|§ 91.131 — Operations in Class B airspace]] | Class B: an ATC clearance is required before entry; the PIC must hold at least a private pilot certificate (or be a student, recreational or sport pilot with the required training and endorsement, except at the airports listed in appendix D section 4); the aircraft needs a two-way radio, a Mode C transponder and ADS-B Out. |
| [[91.133|§ 91.133 — Restricted and prohibited areas]] | You may not fly in a restricted area contrary to its restrictions, or in a prohibited area at all, without the permission of the using or controlling agency. |
| [[91.135|§ 91.135 — Operations in Class A airspace]] | Class A airspace (18,000 ft MSL to FL600) is IFR only: an ATC clearance before entry, two-way radio with ATC, and a transponder and ADS-B Out. Deviations are possible only by ATC authorization. |
| [[91.137|§ 91.137 — Temporary flight restrictions in the vicinity of disaster/hazard areas]] | Temporary flight restrictions for disasters and hazards. A NOTAM designates the area for one of three reasons: protect people from a surface hazard, provide a safe environment for relief aircraft, or prevent sightseeing congestion over an event. Who may enter depends on which paragraph the NOTAM cites; for a sightseeing TFR, flights to or from an airport inside the area and IFR flights may continue. |
| [[91.141|§ 91.141 — Flight restrictions in the proximity of the Presidential and other parties]] | No flight over or near an area the President, Vice President or other public figures are visiting, contrary to the restrictions the FAA publishes by NOTAM. |
| [[91.145|§ 91.145 — Management of aircraft operations in the vicinity of aerial demonstrations and major sporting events]] | TFRs for aerial demonstrations and major sporting events (airshows, the Super Bowl, the Indy 500 and similar). The FAA publishes a NOTAM at least 30 days ahead when it can; the restricted airspace is normally limited to a 3 nm radius for sporting events, and no one may operate inside it except as the NOTAM allows or with ATC or waiver authorization. |
| [[91.151|§ 91.151 — Fuel requirements for flight in VFR conditions]] | Day VFR: enough fuel to fly to the first point of intended landing and then for at least 30 minutes at normal cruise; at night, at least 45 minutes. |
| [[91.153|§ 91.153 — VFR flight plan: Information required]] | What a VFR flight plan must contain: aircraft identification, type, PIC name and address, departure point and time, route, cruising altitude and true airspeed, first intended landing point and ETE, fuel on board in hours, persons aboard, and anything else ATC needs. Once activated, you must notify an FSS or ATC when you cancel or complete the flight. |
| [[91.155|§ 91.155 — Basic VFR weather minimums]] | Basic VFR weather minimums by airspace: 3 statute miles and 500 ft below, 1,000 ft above, 2,000 ft horizontal from clouds in Classes C, D and E below 10,000 ft; Class B is 3 miles clear of clouds; Class G by day below 1,200 ft AGL is 1 mile clear of clouds; at or above 10,000 ft MSL it is 5 miles and 1,000 below, 1,000 above, 1 mile horizontal. Plus: no VFR beneath a ceiling below 1,000 ft in a surface area, and 3 miles ground visibility to take off, land or enter the pattern there. |
| [[91.157|§ 91.157 — Special VFR weather minimums]] | Special VFR lets you operate in a surface area below basic VFR minimums with an ATC clearance, clear of clouds, with at least 1 statute mile flight visibility (and 1 mile ground visibility to take off or land); between sunrise and sunset unless you are instrument rated and the aircraft is equipped for IFR. |
| [[91.159|§ 91.159 — VFR cruising altitude or flight level]] | VFR cruising altitudes apply in level flight more than 3,000 ft above the surface: on a magnetic course of 0° through 179° fly an odd thousand-foot MSL altitude plus 500 ft; on 180° through 359° fly an even thousand plus 500 ft. |
| [[91.203|§ 91.203 — Civil aircraft: Certifications required]] | The paperwork that must be in the airplane: an appropriate and current airworthiness certificate, displayed at the cabin or cockpit entrance where it is legible, and an effective U.S. registration certificate (or the temporary alternatives the section lists). A fuel tank in the cabin needs a Form 337 aboard. |
| [[91.205|§ 91.205 — Powered civil aircraft with standard U.S. airworthiness certificates: Instrument and equipment requirements]] | The instruments and equipment that must be installed and operable: paragraph (b) for day VFR (airspeed, altimeter, compass, tachometer, oil pressure and temperature gauges, fuel gauges, gear indicator, belts and harnesses, ELT and more), paragraph (c) adds for night VFR position lights, an anticollision light system, an adequate electrical source, spare fuses and a landing light if for hire; paragraph (d) is the IFR list. Anything inoperative goes through § 91.213. |
| [[91.207|§ 91.207 — Emergency locator transmitters]] | Most U.S. airplanes must carry an operable automatic ELT; batteries are replaced after more than 1 cumulative hour of use or at 50 percent of their useful life, the ELT is inspected within 12 calendar months, and exemptions include training flights within 50 NM of the departure airport and up to 90 days with the ELT removed for repair. |
| [[91.209|§ 91.209 — Aircraft lights]] | From sunset to sunrise an aircraft must show lighted position lights, and an aircraft equipped with anticollision lights must have them on at all times unless the PIC decides safety requires them off. |
| [[91.211|§ 91.211 — Supplemental oxygen]] | Supplemental oxygen by cabin pressure altitude: the required crew must use it above 12,500 ft MSL for any part of the flight over 30 minutes, at all times above 14,000 ft, and every occupant must be provided with it above 15,000 ft. |
| [[91.213|§ 91.213 — Inoperative instruments and equipment]] | Flying with inoperative equipment: without an MEL, a small non-turbine airplane may fly if the item is not required by the day-VFR type certification list, the aircraft's equipment list or KOEL, § 91.205 or any other rule for that kind of flight, or an AD; it is removed, or deactivated and placarded “Inoperative”, with maintenance recorded; and a pilot or mechanic determines it is no hazard. |
| [[91.215|§ 91.215 — ATC transponder and altitude reporting equipment and use]] | A Mode C transponder is required in Class A, B and C airspace, within the 30 NM Mode C veil of Class B primary airports from the surface to 10,000 ft, above Class B and C to 10,000 ft, and at and above 10,000 ft MSL except within 2,500 ft of the surface; when installed and operable it must be on and replying in all controlled airspace. ADS-B Out (§ 91.225) covers the same footprint. |
| [[91.225|§ 91.225 — Automatic Dependent Surveillance-Broadcast (ADS-B) Out equipment and use]] | Where ADS-B Out is required: Class A; Class B and C; within 30 nm of the Appendix D airports (the Mode C veil) from the surface to 10,000 ft MSL; above the ceiling of Class B or C up to 10,000 ft; Class E at and above 10,000 ft MSL except at or below 2,500 ft AGL; and over the Gulf at and above 3,000 ft MSL out to 12 nm. Aircraft without an engine-driven electrical system are exempt from some of it. Equipped aircraft transmit at all times; an unequipped aircraft must request an ATC deviation at least an hour ahead. |
| [[91.403|§ 91.403 — General]] | The owner or operator is primarily responsible for keeping the aircraft airworthy, including compliance with airworthiness directives (Part 39), and maintenance may only be done as Part 43 and this subpart prescribe. |
| [[91.405|§ 91.405 — Maintenance required]] | Owner and operator duties: have the required inspections done and discrepancies repaired between them, make sure maintenance personnel record the return to service, have inoperative equipment permitted by § 91.213(d) repaired, replaced, removed or inspected at the next required inspection, and make sure inoperative instruments and equipment are placarded. |
| [[91.407|§ 91.407 — Operation after maintenance, preventive maintenance, rebuilding, or alteration]] | After maintenance the aircraft may not be operated until it is approved for return to service by an authorized person and the record entry is made. If the work may have appreciably changed the flight characteristics, no one other than crew may be carried until an appropriately rated pilot with at least a private pilot certificate flies it and logs a satisfactory operational check, unless ground tests prove the check unnecessary. |
| [[91.409|§ 91.409 — Inspections]] | An annual inspection within the preceding 12 calendar months is required before any operation; an aircraft carrying persons for hire, or used to give flight instruction for hire, also needs a 100-hour inspection, which may be exceeded by up to 10 hours to reach a place where it can be done. |
| [[91.411|§ 91.411 — Altimeter system and altitude reporting equipment tests and inspections]] | For IFR in controlled airspace, the static system, each altimeter and the automatic altitude-reporting system must have been tested and inspected within the preceding 24 calendar months, and again after the static system is opened or the encoder worked on. |
| [[91.413|§ 91.413 — ATC transponder tests and inspections]] | Any ATC transponder you use must have been tested and inspected within the preceding 24 calendar months, and re-tested after any installation or maintenance that could introduce a data error. Only a rated repair station, a continuous-airworthiness program holder or the manufacturer may do it. |
| [[91.417|§ 91.417 — Maintenance records]] | Maintenance records the owner or operator must keep: records of maintenance, preventive maintenance, alterations and inspections (description, date, signature and certificate number), plus the running status records (total time, life-limited parts, overhaul times, inspection status, AD status with method of compliance, and Form 337s). The first set is kept for a year or until the work is repeated; the status records stay with the aircraft when sold. Records must be available to the FAA and NTSB. |
| [[1-1-2|AIM 1-1-2 — Nondirectional Radio Beacon (NDB)]] | The NDB is a low or medium frequency beacon (190 to 535 kHz) that an ADF receiver can home on or take bearings from. It gives no course guidance of its own, only a bearing to the station, and it is vulnerable to lightning, precipitation static and, at night, interference from distant stations. |
| [[1-1-3|AIM 1-1-3 — VHF Omni-directional Range (VOR)]] | The VOR: 108.0 to 117.95 MHz, line-of-sight, course accuracy of about plus or minus 1 degree, identified only by its Morse code or recorded voice ident. Propeller RPM can make the needle wander up to 6 degrees. The VOR Minimum Operational Network keeps enough stations that a MON airport with a non-GPS approach is always within 100 nm and VOR coverage is continuous at 5,000 ft AGL. |
| [[1-1-4|AIM 1-1-4 — VOR Receiver Check]] | How to check a VOR receiver: a VOT test signal reads 0 degrees FROM or 180 TO; ground checkpoints allow plus or minus 4 degrees, airborne checkpoints and airway checks plus or minus 6, and two independent receivers checked against each other may differ by 4 degrees. Checkpoint and VOT locations are in the Chart Supplement, and the check is required before IFR flight by § 91.171. |
| [[1-1-7|AIM 1-1-7 — Distance Measuring Equipment (DME)]] | DME measures the round-trip time of paired pulses between aircraft and ground station and shows slant-range distance, not horizontal distance. It is line-of-sight, usable to 199 nm with accuracy of a half mile or 3 percent, and it is paired with the VOR or localizer frequency so you tune one and get both. |
| [[1-1-8|AIM 1-1-8 — NAVAID Service Volumes]] | Standard service volumes tell you how far from a NAVAID the signal is protected: Terminal class out to 25 nm from 1,000 to 12,000 ft above the transmitter, Low class 40 nm up to 18,000 ft, High class 40, 100 or 130 nm depending on altitude, plus the newer VOR Low and VOR High classes that extend to 70 nm for the MON. NDB ranges are by class: compass locator 15, MH 25, H 50, HH 75 nm at any altitude. The cone directly above a NAVAID is unusable. |
| [[1-1-13|AIM 1-1-13 — User Reports Requested on NAVAID Outages]] | Pilots are asked to report NAVAID malfunctions and GPS anomalies (interference, jamming, spoofing) promptly: switch to another navigation source, tell ATC if you need an amended clearance, and file a detailed report with the time, location, aircraft and equipment. GPS anomalies inside a known testing NOTAM area need not be reported in detail unless something unexpected happens. |
| [[1-1-17|AIM 1-1-17 — Global Positioning System (GPS)]] | GPS for pilots: the constellation is designed so at least five satellites are visible, four are needed for a 3D position, and RAIM needs five (or four plus baro-aiding) to detect a bad satellite. For VFR the AIM's cautions are the point: VFR and hand-held receivers have no RAIM alerting, databases need not be current for VFR but should be, antenna placement matters, and you must not rely on GPS alone. VFR waypoints are five-letter names starting with VP, not for use with ATC and not allowed on IFR flight plans. The IFR half covers approved equipment, database and approach requirements. |
| [[1-1-18|AIM 1-1-18 — Wide Area Augmentation System (WAAS)]] | WAAS augments GPS with ground reference stations and a geostationary correction signal, improving accuracy, integrity and availability enough for approaches with vertical guidance (LPV as low as 200 ft and a half mile). A WAAS receiver does not need other navigation equipment for the route, and WAAS NOTAMs use the words MAY NOT BE AVBL to warn of degraded vertical guidance. |
| [[2-1-4|AIM 2-1-4 — Runway Edge Light Systems]] | Runway edge lights come in high, medium and low intensity. They are white, except that on instrument runways the last 2,000 ft or half the runway (whichever is less) is yellow as a caution zone. The lights at the runway end show red to the departing aircraft and green outward to the landing aircraft as the threshold. |
| [[2-1-5|AIM 2-1-5 — In-runway Lighting]] | Lights set into the runway pavement: centerline lights every 50 ft that are white, then alternate red and white in the last 3,000 ft, then all red in the last 1,000; touchdown zone lights; lead-off and lead-on taxiway lights alternating green and yellow at the runway boundary; and land-and-hold-short lights, a row of pulsing white lights at the hold-short point when LAHSO is in effect. |
| [[2-1-6|AIM 2-1-6 — Runway Status Light (RWSL) System]] | Runway Status Lights are automatic red lights driven by surveillance: Runway Entrance Lights at the hold line mean the runway is unsafe to enter or cross, and Takeoff Hold Lights in the runway mean it is unsafe to take off. Never cross illuminated red lights; if they conflict with your clearance, stop and ask ATC. When they go out, that is not a clearance. |
| [[2-1-8|AIM 2-1-8 — Pilot Control of Airport Lighting]] | At airports without a tower or after hours, the runway and approach lights can be turned on from the air by keying the microphone on the published frequency (usually the CTAF): 7 clicks within 5 seconds for highest intensity, 5 for medium, 3 for lowest, and the lights stay on for 15 minutes. All radio-controlled systems at one airport share the same frequency, so keying 7 first then stepping down is the recommended technique. |
| [[2-1-9|AIM 2-1-9 — Airport/Heliport Beacons]] | Airport beacons flash 24 to 30 times a minute (30 to 45 for heliports). White and green is a lighted land airport, white and yellow a water airport, green-yellow-white a heliport, and a military airport shows two quick white flashes between the greens. In a Class B, C, D or E surface area a beacon running during the day often means the weather is below VFR, but do not rely on it alone. |
| [[2-1-10|AIM 2-1-10 — Taxiway Lights]] | Taxiway lights by color: blue edge lights, green centerline lights, yellow clearance bars at holding positions, flashing yellow runway guard lights at runway intersections, and red stop bars that you never cross even with a clearance until they are turned off. |
| [[2-2-1|AIM 2-2-1 — Aeronautical Light Beacons]] | An aeronautical light beacon is a flashing white or colored light that marks an airport, heliport, landmark, a Federal airway point in mountainous terrain, or an obstruction; the color combination tells you which. |
| [[2-2-3|AIM 2-2-3 — Obstruction Lights]] | Obstructions are marked with flashing red beacons (20 to 40 flashes a minute) and steady red lights at night, with orange and white paint by day; medium- or high-intensity white strobes may replace paint and red lights; and catenary lighting marks high-voltage lines across rivers and gorges. |
| [[2-3-3|AIM 2-3-3 — Runway Markings]] | Runway markings: visual, non-precision and precision runways carry progressively more elements. The runway number is the magnetic azimuth to the nearest 10 degrees; the aiming point bars sit about 1,000 ft from the threshold; touchdown zone bars count 500 ft increments; threshold stripes reflect runway width. A displaced threshold's pavement may be used for taxi, takeoff, and landing from the opposite direction, but not for landing on; chevrons mark pavement unusable for anything. |
| [[2-3-4|AIM 2-3-4 — Taxiway Markings]] | Taxiway markings are yellow: a single continuous centerline, an enhanced centerline of dashes on both sides for 150 ft before a runway holding position, a continuous double yellow edge line you must not cross, a dashed double yellow edge line you may cross onto usable pavement, and surface-painted signs and geographic position markings at some airports. |
| [[2-3-5|AIM 2-3-5 — Holding Position Markings]] | Holding position markings: four yellow lines, two solid and two dashed, across the taxiway; the solid lines are on the side where you hold, and you must stop so no part of the aircraft crosses the marking unless cleared. Markings on runways are for LAHSO and taxi. ILS critical-area markings are a yellow ladder of two solid lines with rungs. A single dashed line across a taxiway is a taxiway-to-taxiway hold point, used only when ATC says hold short of that taxiway. |
| [[2-3-7|AIM 2-3-7 — Airport Signs]] | Six kinds of airport signs: mandatory instruction, location, direction, destination, information, and runway distance remaining. Each has its own colors and the following paragraphs describe them. |
| [[2-3-8|AIM 2-3-8 — Mandatory Instruction Signs]] | Mandatory instruction signs are white on red: runway holding position signs (the numbers are arranged so the runway threshold on the left is the left number, e.g. 15-33), runway approach-area holding signs, ILS critical area signs, and the no-entry sign. They mark where you must stop without clearance. |
| [[2-3-9|AIM 2-3-9 — Location Signs]] | Location signs are yellow on black and tell you which taxiway or runway you are on. Two related boundary signs face the runway: the runway boundary sign (yellow with the hold-line graphic) and the ILS critical area boundary sign; when you can read them you are clear of the runway or the critical area. |
| [[2-3-10|AIM 2-3-10 — Direction Signs]] | Direction signs are black on yellow with an arrow for each intersecting taxiway, normally placed on the left before the intersection and arranged clockwise from the first taxiway on your left, with the location sign in the middle separating left turns from straight-ahead and right turns. |
| [[3-1-1|AIM 3-1-1 — General]] | The airspace taxonomy in one paragraph: two categories (regulatory, meaning Classes A through E plus restricted and prohibited areas; and nonregulatory, meaning MOAs, warning areas, alert areas, controlled firing areas and national security areas) and four types: controlled, uncontrolled, special use and other. |
| [[3-1-3|AIM 3-1-3 — Hierarchy of Overlapping Airspace Designations]] | When two airspace designations overlap, the rules of the more restrictive one apply, and the AIM ranks them: A over B over C over D over E over G. |
| [[3-1-4|AIM 3-1-4 — Basic VFR Weather Minimums]] | The AIM's copy of the basic VFR weather minimums table from § 91.155, with two reminders: student pilots also have their own limits in § 61.89, and VFR beneath a ceiling below 1,000 ft in a surface area is prohibited without special VFR. |
| [[3-1-5|AIM 3-1-5 — VFR Cruising Altitudes and Flight Levels]] | VFR cruising altitudes above 3,000 ft AGL and below 18,000 ft MSL: magnetic course 0 to 179 degrees, odd thousands plus 500; 180 to 359, even thousands plus 500. Above 18,000 the same pattern applies to flight levels, but that is Class A and IFR. |
| [[3-2-1|AIM 3-2-1 — General]] | Controlled airspace basics: ATC service is provided to IFR flights and to VFR flights according to the class. It is the pilot's responsibility to meet the clearance or communication requirement before entering Class B, C or D, even while receiving radar advisories. Traffic advisories are workload permitting; safety alerts are mandatory. Ultralights, unmanned balloons and parachute jumps need authorization in the controlled classes. |
| [[3-2-2|AIM 3-2-2 — Class A Airspace]] | Class A: from 18,000 ft MSL up to and including FL600, including the airspace over the waters out to 12 nm; IFR only unless otherwise authorized; not charted. |
| [[3-2-3|AIM 3-2-3 — Class B Airspace]] | Class B surrounds the busiest airports, typically from the surface to 10,000 ft MSL in tailored layers; an ATC clearance is required to enter, VFR aircraft receive separation inside, cloud clearance is simply clear of clouds, and the 30 NM Mode C veil surrounds the primary airport. |
| [[3-2-4|AIM 3-2-4 — Class C Airspace]] | Class C is usually a 5 NM core from the surface and a 10 NM shelf from about 1,200 ft, both up to 4,000 ft above the airport, with a 20 NM outer area where services continue but participation is voluntary; two-way communications (your call sign used) before entry, transponder and ADS-B Out required. |
| [[3-2-5|AIM 3-2-5 — Class D Airspace]] | Class D surrounds towered airports, usually from the surface to 2,500 ft above field elevation; two-way communications before entry (your call sign used), no separation services for VFR, and when a part-time tower closes the area reverts to Class E or Class G as the Chart Supplement states. |
| [[3-2-6|AIM 3-2-6 — Class E Airspace]] | Class E is controlled airspace with no communication or clearance requirement for VFR: transition areas begin at 700 ft AGL (magenta vignette) or 1,200 ft AGL (blue vignette) around airports with instrument procedures, Class E otherwise starts at 14,500 ft MSL, and Federal airways are Class E from 1,200 ft AGL up to 18,000 ft. |
| [[3-4-1|AIM 3-4-1 — General]] | Special use airspace confines hazardous activities or limits nonparticipating aircraft. Prohibited and restricted areas are regulatory (Part 73); warning areas, MOAs, alert areas, controlled firing areas and national security areas are not. Permanent SUA is charted with hours, altitudes and controlling agency; CFAs, temporary MOAs and temporary restricted areas are not charted. |
| [[3-4-2|AIM 3-4-2 — Prohibited Areas]] | Prohibited areas are established for national security or welfare and flight inside them is prohibited; they are published in the Federal Register and charted. |
| [[3-4-3|AIM 3-4-3 — Restricted Areas]] | Restricted areas contain invisible hazards such as artillery, aerial gunnery or guided missiles; flight is subject to restrictions rather than prohibited outright. When a joint-use area is not active and released to the FAA, ATC will let IFR aircraft through; VFR pilots must find out the status from the controlling agency or FSS. Permanent restricted areas are charted; temporary ones are not. |
| [[3-4-4|AIM 3-4-4 — Warning Areas]] | A warning area begins 3 nm off the coast and contains activity hazardous to nonparticipating aircraft, over domestic or international waters. Its purpose is to warn; there is no legal bar to entry, but the hazard is the same as a restricted area. |
| [[3-4-5|AIM 3-4-5 — Military Operations Areas]] | MOAs separate military training (air combat, intercepts, aerobatics, low-altitude tactics) from IFR traffic. VFR flight is permitted but the AIM says exercise extreme caution, get the real-time status from an FSS within 100 miles, and contact the controlling agency for advisories before entering an active MOA. Permanent MOAs are charted; temporary ones are not. |
| [[3-4-6|AIM 3-4-6 — Alert Areas]] | Alert areas mark a high volume of pilot training or unusual aerial activity. Everyone flies under the regular rules there and both participants and transiting aircraft share collision-avoidance responsibility. |
| [[3-4-7|AIM 3-4-7 — Controlled Firing Areas]] | Controlled firing areas contain hazardous activity that is suspended the moment spotters, radar or lookouts detect an approaching aircraft, which is why they are not charted. |
| [[3-4-8|AIM 3-4-8 — National Security Areas]] | National security areas surround sensitive ground facilities; pilots are asked to avoid them voluntarily, and when needed flight through them can be prohibited by NOTAM under § 99.7. |
| [[3-5-2|AIM 3-5-2 — Military Training Routes]] | Military training routes carry high-speed low-level training: generally below 10,000 ft MSL at more than 250 knots. IR routes are flown IFR, VR routes VFR with at least 5 miles visibility and a 3,000 ft ceiling. Four-digit routes have no segment above 1,500 ft AGL; three-digit routes do. You are not prohibited from an MTR, but call an FSS within 100 nm for the activity schedule and be vigilant; route width can be several miles either side of the line. |
| [[3-5-3|AIM 3-5-3 — Temporary Flight Restrictions]] | The AIM's operational explanation of temporary flight restrictions: who requests them, which rule each type cites (§ 91.137 hazards, § 91.138 Hawaii, § 91.141 VIPs, § 91.143 space operations), that hazard TFRs are normally 3 nm and 2,000 ft, that incidents inside Class B, C or D are handled by existing procedures, and how the NOTAM text is formatted. |
| [[3-5-6|AIM 3-5-6 — Terminal Radar Service Area (TRSA)]] | A TRSA is not regulatory airspace: the primary airport is Class D, the rest overlies Class E, and participation in TRSA radar service is voluntary. It is charted with a solid black line and segment altitudes, with the Class D core in blue dashes. |
| [[3-5-7|AIM 3-5-7 — Special Air Traffic Rules (SATR) and Special Flight Rules Area (SFRA)]] | Special air traffic rules areas and special flight rules areas are Part 93 airspace with their own rules; anyone operating to, from or within one must follow them unless ATC authorizes otherwise, and SFRAs are charted on sectionals, terminal area and helicopter route charts. |
| [[3-5-8|AIM 3-5-8 — Washington, DC, Special Flight Rules Area (SFRA) including the Flight Restricted Zone (FRZ)]] | The Washington DC SFRA and inner Flight Restricted Zone: comply with § 93.339, complete the § 91.161 special awareness training before flying within 60 nm of the DCA VOR, and watch for § 99.7 security NOTAMs. |
| [[4-1-1|AIM 4-1-1 — Air Route Traffic Control Centers]] | Air Route Traffic Control Centers exist primarily for IFR aircraft in controlled airspace during the en route phase; VFR flight following from Center is a workload-permitting extra. |
| [[4-1-2|AIM 4-1-2 — Control Towers]] | Control towers provide a safe, orderly and expeditious flow of traffic on and around the airport, and where delegated they separate IFR aircraft in the terminal area. |
| [[4-1-3|AIM 4-1-3 — Flight Service Stations]] | Flight Service Stations provide pilot briefings, flight plan processing, en route advisories, search and rescue services and help for lost aircraft and emergencies; they relay clearances, process NOTAMs and broadcast weather. In Alaska some also take observations and give airport advisories. |
| [[4-1-9|AIM 4-1-9 — Traffic Advisory Practices at Airports Without Operating Control Towers]] | Operating at an airport without an operating control tower: use the CTAF (a UNICOM, MULTICOM, FSS or tower frequency listed in the Chart Supplement), use the airport's correct name, and self-announce. Inbound: monitor and report from 10 miles out, entering downwind, base and final, and leaving the runway. Outbound: report before taxiing and before taxiing onto the runway, and monitor until 10 miles out. Where there is no tower, FSS or UNICOM, self-announce on MULTICOM 122.9. |
| [[4-1-11|AIM 4-1-11 — Designated UNICOM/MULTICOM Frequencies]] | The FCC frequency plan: 122.700, 122.725, 122.800, 122.975, 123.000, 123.050 and 123.075 for UNICOM at airports without a tower; 122.900 MULTICOM where there is no tower, FSS or UNICOM; 122.950 UNICOM at towered airports; 122.750 for fixed-wing air-to-air; 123.300 and 123.500 for flight instruction, gliders and balloons. |
| [[4-1-13|AIM 4-1-13 — Automatic Terminal Information Service (ATIS)]] | ATIS is a continuous recorded broadcast of routine airport information (weather, runways in use, approaches, NOTAMs) identified by a phonetic letter. Tell the controller on initial contact that you have the current letter so the information need not be repeated. Ceiling and visibility are omitted when the ceiling is above 5,000 ft and visibility more than 5 miles. Saying 'have numbers' means only wind, runway and altimeter, never the ATIS. |
| [[4-1-15|AIM 4-1-15 — Radar Traffic Information Service]] | Radar traffic information service ('flight following'): the controller points out targets that may conflict, by clock position, distance, direction of travel and type and altitude if known. Clock position is based on your track, so with a crab the traffic appears offset. It is entirely at the controller's discretion, does not include vectors away from traffic unless you ask, and never relieves you of see-and-avoid. Monitor the frequency continuously and, when you no longer want the service, tell the controller before switching and squawk 1200. |
| [[4-1-16|AIM 4-1-16 — Safety Alert]] | A safety alert is issued when the controller sees an aircraft under their control in unsafe proximity to terrain, obstructions or other aircraft: a terrain or obstruction alert (often from Minimum Safe Altitude Warning on Mode C) or an aircraft conflict alert with a suggested heading or altitude. VFR aircraft with Mode C can request MSAW monitoring. |
| [[4-1-17|AIM 4-1-17 — Radar Assistance to VFR Aircraft]] | Radar-equipped facilities can give VFR aircraft vectors when you can communicate, are in radar coverage and are identified. The vectors are advisory: flying safely and legally stays with the pilot, so keep the controller told of the weather you are in and ahead so you are not vectored into IMC. Vectors may be started by the controller with your concurrence, under a published program, or when needed for safety. |
| [[4-1-18|AIM 4-1-18 — Terminal Radar Services for VFR Aircraft]] | Terminal radar services for VFR aircraft come in tiers: basic radar service (safety alerts, traffic advisories, limited vectoring and, at some airports, sequencing); TRSA service (separation and sequencing, voluntary, decline with 'negative TRSA service'); Class C service (separation between IFR and VFR plus sequencing to the primary airport); and Class B service (separation of all aircraft). Contact approach about 25 miles out where sequencing is provided. None of it relieves you of see-and-avoid, wake avoidance, terrain clearance or VFR weather minimums, and the Class B clearance and Class C contact requirements stand even when radar is down. |
| [[4-1-20|AIM 4-1-20 — Transponder and ADS-B Out Operation]] | Squawk 1200 for VFR with altitude reporting on; the transponder and ADS-B Out stay on from pushback to parking, ident only when asked, never squawk 7777, and avoid passing through 7500, 7600 or 7700 when changing codes. |
| [[4-2-1|AIM 4-2-1 — General]] | The single most important thing in radio communication is understanding: acknowledge every ATC transmission with your call sign, be brief but tell the controller what you want, and use the Pilot/Controller Glossary phraseology rather than jargon or CB slang. |
| [[4-2-2|AIM 4-2-2 — Radio Technique]] | Radio technique: listen before you transmit, think before you key, hold the mic close and pause after keying, wait a few seconds before calling again, watch for a stuck mic, and remember VHF is line of sight so altitude extends range. |
| [[4-2-3|AIM 4-2-3 — Contact Procedures]] | Initial contact format: the facility you are calling, your full aircraft identification, your position if on the airport surface, your request if short, and 'over' if needed; add position, altitude and the ATIS letter when reception is good. Acknowledge every frequency change and make it as soon as possible unless told to change at a time, fix or altitude. 'Change to my frequency' means the same controller and an abbreviated call. |
| [[4-2-4|AIM 4-2-4 — Aircraft Call Signs]] | Call signs: never abbreviate on initial contact or when similar call signs are on frequency; ATC may shorten yours to the last three characters and then you may too. Civil aircraft state the type or manufacturer and the registration without the N; air taxis without authorized call signs prefix 'Tango'; MEDEVAC is for urgent medical priority only; and student pilots should say 'student pilot' on initial contact with each facility to get extra help. |
| [[4-2-7|AIM 4-2-7 — Phonetic Alphabet]] | The ICAO phonetic alphabet (Alfa through Zulu) is used when conditions make plain letters hard to copy, and pilots should use it when identifying their aircraft on initial contact and whenever similar call signs are on frequency. |
| [[4-2-8|AIM 4-2-8 — Figures]] | Speaking numbers: round hundreds and thousands up to 9,900 are spoken as words ('four thousand five hundred'); above 9,900 the digits before 'thousand' are separated ('one zero thousand'); airways as 'Victor Twelve'; all other numbers digit by digit; and a frequency's decimal is 'point'. |
| [[4-2-9|AIM 4-2-9 — Altitudes and Flight Levels]] | Altitudes below 18,000 ft are spoken as the separate digits of the thousands plus the hundreds ('one two thousand five hundred'); at and above 18,000 say 'flight level' and the digits ('flight level one niner zero'). |
| [[4-2-13|AIM 4-2-13 — Communications with Tower when Aircraft Transmitter or Receiver or Both are Inoperative]] | Radio failure at a towered airport. Receiver out: stay outside or above the Class D surface area until you have the traffic flow, then tell the tower your type, position, altitude and intentions and ask for light signals. Transmitter out: same, then join the pattern, monitor tower, and watch for light signals, acknowledging by rocking wings by day or blinking the landing light at night. Both out: join the pattern and watch the tower. Departing with a dead radio: phone the tower for authorization. |
| [[4-2-14|AIM 4-2-14 — Communications for VFR Flights]] | Talking to Flight Service in flight: 122.2 is the common en route frequency for most FSSs, with other frequencies listed on the chart; state the frequency you are using and your location on the initial call so the specialist knows which transmitter to answer on. |
| [[4-3-1|AIM 4-3-1 — General]] | Why airport operations get their own section: congestion, climbing and descending traffic, cockpit distraction and marginal weather all pile up near the airport, so the AIM asks for extra alertness there and lays out the rules and practices that follow. |
| [[4-3-2|AIM 4-3-2 — Airports with an Operating Control Tower]] | At a towered airport keep two-way radio contact with the tower inside the Class B, C or D surface area, calling about 15 miles out. Fixed-wing aircraft circle to the left unless the tower says otherwise. The standard pattern legs are departure, upwind, crosswind, downwind, base and final; the departure climb continues to at least a half mile past the runway end and within 300 ft of pattern altitude. Tower radar displays are for the controller's efficiency, and suggested headings from the tower are advisory. |
| [[4-3-3|AIM 4-3-3 — Traffic Patterns]] | The standard traffic pattern: enter in level flight abeam midfield at pattern altitude (1,000 ft AGL for propeller aircraft unless the Chart Supplement says otherwise), all turns left unless RP is charted, turn crosswind beyond the departure end within 300 ft of pattern altitude, complete the turn to final at least 1/4 mile out, depart straight out or with a 45° turn beyond the departure end. |
| [[4-3-4|AIM 4-3-4 — Visual Indicators at Airports Without an Operating Control Tower]] | At a non-towered airport the segmented circle shows the pattern: the wind sock or tee gives wind, a tetrahedron (small end into the landing direction) gives landing direction, landing strip indicators show runway alignment and traffic pattern indicators show which runways use right traffic. Turns are to the left unless an indicator says right. When two aircraft are approaching to land, the lower one has the right-of-way but may not cut in front of one on final. |
| [[4-3-5|AIM 4-3-5 — Unexpected Maneuvers in the Airport Traffic Pattern]] | Controllers sequence traffic on what they observe and what you tell them; an unexpected maneuver (a 360 on final, an unannounced go-around, an early turn) breaks the sequence and causes incidents. Fly what you said you would, and if you must change, say so first. |
| [[4-3-6|AIM 4-3-6 — Use of Runways/Declared Distances]] | Runways are numbered by the magnetic azimuth to the nearest 10 degrees, and the tower's wind is magnetic in knots. ATC assigns the runway most aligned with a wind of 5 knots or more, otherwise the calm-wind runway, unless a runway use program applies; you may request another runway. Declared distances (TORA, TODA, ASDA, LDA) are what the airport operator says is available for takeoff, accelerate-stop and landing, and may be shorter than the pavement. |
| [[4-3-7|AIM 4-3-7 — Low Level Wind Shear/Microburst Detection Systems]] | Airports may have wind shear and microburst detection (LLWAS, TDWR, WSP, ITWS) whose alerts controllers relay: for LLWAS as airport wind followed by a boundary wind, and for the newer systems as a microburst or wind shear alert with the expected airspeed loss and location on final. |
| [[4-3-11|AIM 4-3-11 — Pilot Responsibilities When Conducting Land and Hold Short Operations (LAHSO)]] | Land and hold short operations: ATC may clear you to land and hold short of an intersecting runway, taxiway or point. You may accept only if you can stop within the published Available Landing Distance, you must read back the hold-short instruction, and the PIC may decline. LAHSO needs at least a 1,000 ft ceiling and 3 miles visibility, and the AIM says student pilots and pilots unfamiliar with LAHSO should not participate. |
| [[4-3-13|AIM 4-3-13 — Traffic Control Light Signals]] | The tower light-gun signals: steady green (cleared for takeoff / cleared to land), flashing green (cleared to taxi / return for landing), steady red (stop / give way and continue circling), flashing red (taxi clear of the runway / airport unsafe, do not land), flashing white (return to starting point on the airport), alternating red and green (exercise extreme caution). Acknowledge by day by moving the ailerons or rudder, at night by blinking the landing or navigation lights. At night, turn on a landing light to get the tower's attention. |
| [[4-3-14|AIM 4-3-14 — Communications]] | Departing a towered airport: call ground or clearance delivery before starting engines for taxi and clearance information, stay on ground during taxi and run-up, then switch to tower when ready for takeoff. Ground frequencies are mostly 121.6 to 121.9, so the controller may say 'ground point seven'; if in doubt about a frequency, ask. |
| [[4-3-18|AIM 4-3-18 — Taxiing]] | Taxiing at a towered airport: get approval before moving onto the movement area, state your position when you call, and get a clearance before taxiing on a runway, taking off or landing. Every runway crossing needs an explicit clearance and ATC never says 'cleared' for taxi. Read back the runway assignment, any clearance to enter a runway, and any hold-short or line-up-and-wait instruction. Ask for progressive taxi if unsure. A taxi clearance does not relieve you of avoiding other aircraft. |
| [[4-3-19|AIM 4-3-19 — Taxi During Low Visibility]] | In low visibility the tower may not be able to see you, so tell the controller at the first sign of difficulty or disorientation, use extreme caution taxiing toward the sun, and keep all attention on taxiing: checklists and non-essential radio calls wait until the aircraft is stopped with the brakes set. |
| [[4-3-20|AIM 4-3-20 — Standard Taxi Routes]] | Standard taxi routes are pre-defined, published routes some airports use; you may request one by name if you know it, and requesting one means you are familiar with it. An STR instruction never includes a runway crossing and gives no right of way, and you may decline it or ask for full taxi instructions. |
| [[4-3-21|AIM 4-3-21 — Exiting the Runway After Landing]] | After landing, exit at the first available taxiway or as instructed, without stopping or reversing on the runway. You are clear of the runway only when every part of the aircraft is past the hold-short markings, so taxi beyond them even if that means entering another taxiway. Then change to ground when told and get a taxi clearance; a taxi-to-parking clearance never authorizes entering or crossing a runway. |
| [[4-3-24|AIM 4-3-24 — Use of Aircraft Lights]] | Aircraft lights: position lights are required from sunset to sunrise on the surface and in flight, and anti-collision lights whenever the aircraft operates, day or night, unless the PIC judges them a hazard. Operation Lights On encourages landing lights for takeoff and below 10,000 ft near airports. Turn on nav, beacon and strobes before taxi (strobes off if they blind others), all exterior lights when crossing or entering any runway, and landing lights when cleared for takeoff. |
| [[4-3-27|AIM 4-3-27 — Operations at Uncontrolled Airports With Automated Surface Observing System (ASOS)/Automated Weather Observing System (AWOS)]] | Many non-towered airports have ASOS or AWOS broadcasting one-minute weather, receivable within about 25 nm below 10,000 ft on the frequency in the Chart Supplement. Controllers issuing special VFR or IFR clearances there rely on you to report that weather, so state that you have the one-minute weather when you call. |
| [[4-4-6|AIM 4-4-6 — Special VFR Clearances]] | Special VFR: in a Class B, C, D or E surface area with weather below VFR you can ask for a clearance to enter, leave or operate there, traffic permitting. You must stay clear of clouds with at least 1 statute mile flight visibility (and 1 mile ground visibility to take off or land); helicopters are exempt from the mile. Ask the tower, or in a Class E surface area the nearest tower, FSS or Center. No altitude is assigned because you must stay clear of clouds, and you are responsible for terrain and obstacles. Some Class B and C areas prohibit fixed-wing SVFR, and at night fixed-wing SVFR requires an instrument-rated pilot and an IFR-equipped aircraft. |
| [[4-4-10|AIM 4-4-10 — Adherence to Clearance]] | Once you have a clearance you must follow it unless amended; execute it on receipt. 'Immediately' means urgency; a heading assignment means start the turn promptly. 'At pilot's discretion' lets you start a climb or descent when you like, at any rate, with level-offs, but you may not return to an altitude you have left. If emergency authority is used to deviate, tell ATC as soon as possible and get an amended clearance; if ATC gives you priority you may be asked for a report within 48 hours. The last clearance always supersedes the previous one. |
| [[4-4-15|AIM 4-4-15 — Use of Visual Clearing Procedures and Scanning Techniques]] | Clearing procedures and scanning: check the approach paths before taking the runway, make gentle clearing banks in climbs and descents, break up long straight-and-level periods with clearing turns, avoid descending into the pattern, be vigilant near VORs, verbalize 'clear left, clear right' during training, and raise the wing (high-wing) or lower it (low-wing) before turning. Know the scan's limits: instruments and tablets, blind spots, sun and haze, and the eye's refocus time; ADS-B In helps but does not show unequipped aircraft. |
| [[4-5-7|AIM 4-5-7 — Automatic Dependent Surveillance-Broadcast (ADS-B) Services]] | ADS-B basics: the aircraft broadcasts its GPS position about once a second on 978 MHz (UAT) or 1090 MHz (1090ES); ADS-B Out is required in the § 91.225 airspace and above 18,000 ft it must be 1090ES. The transmitted flight ID must match the flight plan call sign. UAT anonymous mode may be used only when not on an IFR flight plan and not requesting ATC services, with 1200 set. Keep the transponder and ADS-B on whenever operating. Unequipped aircraft may only enter ADS-B airspace with a pre-flight deviation, and if ATC issues a clearance into it you must say you are unequipped. ADS-B In traffic is for visual acquisition only, never for avoidance maneuvers. |
| [[4-5-9|AIM 4-5-9 — Flight Information Service- Broadcast (FIS-B)]] | FIS-B is the free weather and aeronautical data broadcast over the 978 MHz UAT link within line of sight of a ground station: METARs, TAFs, NEXRAD, AIRMETs, SIGMETs, PIREPs, TFRs and NOTAMs, each with its own update interval. It does not replace a preflight briefing or in-flight updates from FSS or ATC, and the products have known latency. |
| [[5-1-1|AIM 5-1-1 — Preflight Preparation]] | Preflight preparation: gather all information vital to the flight, decide whether it is safe, then file a flight plan. Self-briefing from automated sources is regulatory-compliant (see AC 91-92); if you call Flight Service, self-brief first and give them the type of flight, aircraft, departure, route, destination, altitude, ETD and ETE. Briefers must give NOTAMs in a standard briefing but not Domestic or International Notices unless asked. Use only current charts: sectionals are revised every 56 days. |
| [[5-1-3|AIM 5-1-3 — Notice to Airmen (NOTAM) System]] | The NOTAM system carries time-critical information not yet on charts. § 91.103 requires you to know it before flight. NOTAM (D) covers airports and navaids (runways, taxiways, lighting, obstructions); FDC NOTAMs are regulatory: chart and procedure amendments, TFRs, GPS and WAAS status, laser activity and special notices. Also review the ARTCC NOTAMs for the centers you cross, pointer NOTAMs, and GPS testing NOTAMs, and get destination updates en route. |
| [[5-1-5|AIM 5-1-5 — Flight Plan - VFR Flights]] | VFR flight plans: not required inside the continental U.S. except for DVFR into an ADIZ, but strongly recommended for search and rescue. File FAA Form 7233-4 (the international form), activate it with Flight Service by radio or electronically (towers do not), file a separate plan for each leg, and know that the proposal is held only two hours after the proposed departure time. An assumed departure time can activate it automatically but then you must update any changes. |
| [[5-1-12|AIM 5-1-12 — Change in Flight Plan]] | A change in flight plan includes altitude, route, destination and also speed: tell ATC when your true airspeed at cruise differs from the filed value by 5 percent or 10 knots, whichever is greater. Changes to a filed plan should be made with the original provider more than 46 minutes before departure; closer than that, through ATC or FSS. |
| [[5-1-13|AIM 5-1-13 — Change in Proposed Departure Time]] | Filed flight plans that are never activated are deleted, usually 2 hours after the proposed departure time, so if you will be delayed 2 hours or more, revise the departure time with Flight Service or your filing service rather than over the ATC frequency. |
| [[5-1-14|AIM 5-1-14 — Closing VFR/DVFR Flight Plans]] | You are responsible for closing your VFR or DVFR flight plan with the nearest FSS or, through any ATC facility, by relay. Towers do not close them because they do not know who is on one. If the plan is not closed within a half hour after your ETA, search and rescue begins. |
| [[5-6-13|AIM 5-6-13 — Interception Procedures]] | Military interception: NORAD fighters or helicopters may intercept to identify, track, inspect, divert or establish communications with an aircraft. Monitor 121.5 (guard); if intercepted, contact ATC immediately, follow the visual signals, and squawk 7700 unless told otherwise. Fighters approach from behind; if you do not comply they may fly across your path to divert you. Comply until positively released. |
| [[5-6-14|AIM 5-6-14 — Interception Signals]] | The ICAO interception signals: the interceptor rocking wings (with lights flashing at night) means you have been intercepted, follow me; you respond by rocking wings and following. A climbing breakaway turn of 90 degrees or more means you may proceed. Circling the airport, lowering the gear and overflying the runway means land here; you lower the gear, follow and land. Your own signals: raising the gear and flashing landing lights over the runway means the airfield is inadequate; regular switching of lights means cannot comply; irregular flashing means in distress. |
| [[6-1-1|AIM 6-1-1 — Pilot Responsibility and Authority]] | The PIC is directly responsible for and the final authority on the operation of the aircraft, and in an emergency requiring immediate action may deviate from any rule of Part 91 Subparts A and B to the extent required. If that authority is used to deviate from a clearance, notify ATC as soon as possible and get an amended clearance. |
| [[6-1-2|AIM 6-1-2 — Emergency Condition- Request Assistance Immediately]] | An emergency is a distress or an urgency condition. Pilots declare distress (fire, structural failure) readily but hesitate over urgency; the AIM says you are in at least an urgency condition the moment you become doubtful about position, fuel, weather or anything else that could affect safety. Ask for help immediately: delay has cost lives. |
| [[6-2-1|AIM 6-2-1 — Radar Service for VFR Aircraft in Difficulty]] | Radar can help a VFR pilot in difficulty with vectors, but the guidance is advisory and does not authorize violating the rules. Many non-instrument pilots lose control in clouds, so keep the controller told of the weather; if a VFR option exists take it rather than accept a vector into IMC; if VFR is not possible, tell the controller you are not instrument rated and declare a distress condition. |
| [[6-2-2|AIM 6-2-2 — Transponder Emergency Operation]] | In distress or urgency, squawk 7700 with Mode C to alert the radar facility, then establish communications as soon as possible; keep squawking because you may be outside radar coverage. |
| [[6-2-4|AIM 6-2-4 — Emergency Locator Transmitter (ELT)]] | Emergency locator transmitters are required on most GA airplanes (§ 91.207). Older units transmit on 121.5 and 243.0 MHz, which satellites stopped monitoring in 2009; 406 MHz units are digital, registered with NOAA, and detected almost instantly. Armed ELTs activate on crash forces and run at least 48 hours. Test analog units only during the first 5 minutes after the hour, for no more than three sweeps; 406 units per the manufacturer; never airborne. Monitor 121.5 before shutdown to catch an accidental activation. |
| [[6-2-6|AIM 6-2-6 — Search and Rescue]] | Search and rescue is coordinated by the Coast Guard and Air Force rescue coordination centers. A filed flight plan is the most timely indicator that you are overdue; an Air Force study found 36 hours pass before family concern starts a search when there is no plan. File to the first landing point and refile each leg, report a landing elsewhere or a delay over 30 minutes to FSS, and close the plan on arrival. Survival equipment and body signals for rescue aircraft round out the paragraph. |
| [[6-3-1|AIM 6-3-1 — Distress and Urgency Communications]] | Distress and urgency communications: start with MAYDAY (distress) or PAN-PAN (urgency), preferably three times. MAYDAY has absolute priority and silences the frequency; PAN-PAN has priority over everything but distress. Call the facility you are already talking to, otherwise the one responsible for the area, and if needed use 121.5 or 243.0, which towers, radar facilities and direction-finding stations guard. |
| [[6-3-2|AIM 6-3-2 — Obtaining Emergency Assistance]] | Obtaining emergency assistance: climb if possible for better radio and radar reception; keep the assigned squawk if in contact, otherwise squawk 7700; then transmit MAYDAY or PAN-PAN with the station, your identification and type, nature of the problem, weather, intentions, position and heading, altitude, fuel in minutes, people on board and anything else useful. Then comply with instructions, do not change frequency unless necessary, and if a crash or ditching is imminent give the extra items, turn on the ELT, and stay with the aircraft afterward. |
| [[6-3-3|AIM 6-3-3 — Ditching Procedures]] | Ditching: success depends on sea and wind first, aircraft type second, pilot skill third. Read the swell, land parallel to the primary swell on its crest or back side (never into the face), accept more crosswind to avoid it, judge wind from the streaks on the water, and touch down at the lowest safe speed with the nose up; with no power, carry extra speed to the flare. |
| [[6-4-1|AIM 6-4-1 — Two‐way Radio Communications Failure]] | Two-way radio failure: whether it is an emergency is the pilot's call, and § 91.3(b) allows deviation as needed. Under IFR, § 91.185 governs: in VFR conditions, or when VFR is encountered, continue VFR and land as soon as practicable; in IFR conditions fly the route assigned, vectored, expected or filed, at the highest of the assigned, minimum IFR or expected altitude for each segment, and leave the clearance limit at the expect-further-clearance time or ETA. |
| [[6-4-2|AIM 6-4-2 — Transponder Operation During Two‐way Communications Failure]] | If you lose two-way radio capability, set the transponder to 7600 so ATC sees it, remembering you may be outside radar coverage. |
| [[7-1-1|AIM 7-1-1 — National Weather Service Aviation Weather Service Program]] | Aviation weather is a joint NOAA, NWS, FAA and DoD effort with the FAA as the meteorological authority. The NWS runs the observing network and forecasts: automated METARs and SPECIs, TAFs for over 700 airports valid 24 or 30 hours, SIGMETs and AIRMETs from the Aviation Weather Center and its Alaska and Hawaii counterparts, and the specialized centers (SPC, NHC, SWPC, WPC). The Aviation Weather Handbook, FAA-H-8083-28, describes the products. |
| [[7-1-2|AIM 7-1-2 — FAA Weather Services]] | FAA weather services: Flight Service by phone at 1-800-WX-BRIEF or online at 1800wxbrief.com, and the automated surface observations (METAR) with human augmentation at larger airports. The SPECI table lists what triggers a special report: a wind shift of 45 degrees or more in under 15 minutes at 10 knots or more, visibility crossing 3, 2, 1, half or quarter mile, ceilings crossing 3,000, 1,500, 1,000 or 500 ft, a thunderstorm beginning or ending, tornadoes, hail, freezing precipitation, squalls, and aircraft mishaps. |
| [[7-1-3|AIM 7-1-3 — Use of Aviation Weather Products]] | Weather information comes in three types: observations (raw sensor data), analysis (interpreted observations) and forecasts. Approved sources are the Federal Government (FAA and NWS), FAA-authorized Enhanced Weather Information Systems for certificate holders, and commercial providers who repackage or add to government products. Part 91 operators are encouraged to use FAA and NWS products, must base every decision on all available pertinent products, and should check what a third-party source actually provides. |
| [[7-1-4|AIM 7-1-4 — Graphical Forecasts for Aviation (GFA)]] | The Graphical Forecasts for Aviation website (aviationweather.gov/gfa) replaced the text area forecast: gridded clouds, ceiling and visibility, precipitation, thunderstorms, winds, turbulence and icing from 14 hours in the past to 15 hours ahead, with observation tabs for METARs, PIREPs and radar and satellite, plus static Aviation Cloud Forecast and Aviation Surface Forecast images. |
| [[7-1-5|AIM 7-1-5 — Preflight Briefing]] | Preflight briefings from Flight Service: a Standard briefing when you have had no prior information (adverse conditions, a 'VFR flight not recommended' advisory when the briefer judges VFR doubtful, synopsis, current conditions if departing within 2 hours, en route forecast, destination forecast, winds aloft in knots and degrees true, NOTAMs, ATC delays, and on request SUA status, density altitude and RAIM); an Abbreviated briefing to update or fill gaps; and an Outlook briefing when departure is six or more hours away. |
| [[7-1-6|AIM 7-1-6 — Inflight Aviation Weather Advisories]] | In-flight advisories. SIGMETs (non-convective) cover severe icing, severe or extreme turbulence or clear air turbulence, dust or sand storms below 3 miles, and volcanic ash; valid 4 hours (6 for tropical cyclones and ash). Convective SIGMETs cover severe thunderstorms (winds 50 knots or more, hail 3/4 inch or more, tornadoes), embedded storms, lines, and heavy precipitation over 40 percent of at least 3,000 square miles; any convective SIGMET implies severe turbulence, severe icing and low-level wind shear; issued hourly at H+55 for the western, central and eastern U.S. AIRMETs (Sierra for IFR and mountain obscuration, Tango for turbulence, surface winds and wind shear, Zulu for icing and freezing level) are lower-intensity, scheduled every 6 hours. Center Weather Advisories are the ARTCC's short-term product. |
| [[7-1-8|AIM 7-1-8 — Inflight Weather Advisory Broadcasts]] | When a SIGMET, convective SIGMET, AIRMET (outside the contiguous U.S.), urgent PIREP or Center Weather Advisory affects airspace within 150 miles of their area, Centers broadcast an alert once on all frequencies and tell you to contact Flight Service for details; towers and approach controls may limit their alerts to 50 miles. |
| [[7-1-9|AIM 7-1-9 — Flight Information Services (FIS)]] | Flight Information Services put weather and aeronautical data on cockpit displays via the FAA's free FIS-B (978 MHz UAT) or commercial data links. It supplements, never replaces, voice communication with FSS and ATC; it supports strategic decisions (20 minutes or more) not tactical weather avoidance (under 3 minutes); TFR and NOTAM depictions are for awareness only; and FIS-B NOTAMs are limited to those issued within the past 30 days. |
| [[7-1-10|AIM 7-1-10 — Weather Observing Programs]] | Automated weather observing systems (AWOS) broadcast minute-by-minute weather on a VHF frequency or NAVAID voice, receivable to about 25 nm and 10,000 ft AGL. Levels run from AWOS-A (altimeter only) through AWOS-3 (adds visibility and ceiling) to AWOS-3P, 3PT, 3T and 4 with precipitation, lightning and runway sensors. Automated reports carry the prefix AUTO, the lowest visibility reported is 'less than one quarter', and the word 'clear' is replaced by 'no clouds below' or 'clear below' the sensor's range. |
| [[7-1-12|AIM 7-1-12 — ATC Inflight Weather Avoidance Assistance]] | ATC radar shows precipitation intensity (light under 26 dBZ, moderate 26 to 40, heavy over 40 to 50, extreme over 50) but cannot detect turbulence, and Center's mosaic can be up to 6 minutes old. Controllers will describe weather and help you avoid it when asked: request a deviation with a heading or degrees, direction and miles, a new route, an altitude change or vectors, as early as possible with where you want to start, the extent, and where you will rejoin. IFR pilots must not deviate without a clearance except under emergency authority. |
| [[7-1-18|AIM 7-1-18 — Pilot Weather Reports (PIREPs)]] | Pilot weather reports: ATC must solicit them when ceilings are at or below 5,000 ft, visibility at or below 5 miles, thunderstorms, light or greater icing, moderate or greater turbulence, wind shear or volcanic ash are reported or forecast, and pilots are urged to volunteer them. Give them to whoever you are talking to. Format: UA routine or UUA urgent, then /OV location, /TM time, /FL altitude, /TP type, then sky, weather, temperature, wind, turbulence and icing; the altitude and type are essential for turbulence and icing reports. |
| [[7-1-19|AIM 7-1-19 — PIREPs Relating to Airframe Icing]] | Airframe icing: ice cuts thrust and lift, adds drag and weight, and raises stall speed; a half inch can cut lift by half. Expect icing in visible precipitation between +2 and -10 degrees C; if it starts, leave the precipitation or find warmer air. Intensities: trace, light, moderate and severe, defined by accretion rate and how the deice systems cope. Rime ice is rough and opaque from small droplets freezing instantly; clear ice is glossy from large droplets freezing slowly. |
| [[7-1-20|AIM 7-1-20 — Definitions of Inflight Icing Terms]] | The AIM's icing vocabulary: glaze (clear) ice, rime ice, mixed ice, intercycle, residual and runback ice; and the conditions: known icing (ice observed or detected in flight), forecast icing, potential icing (the manufacturer's temperature-and-moisture definition), freezing drizzle and freezing rain, supercooled drops and supercooled large drops. |
| [[7-1-21|AIM 7-1-21 — PIREPs Relating to Turbulence]] | Turbulence reporting: light (slight, erratic changes; slight strain on belts), moderate (definite changes, aircraft in positive control; definite strains, objects dislodged), severe (large abrupt changes, momentarily out of control; occupants forced violently against belts) and extreme (practically impossible to control, possible structural damage). 'Chop' is the rhythmic bumpiness without attitude change. Occasional is less than a third of the time, intermittent a third to two thirds, continuous more. Report location, time, intensity, in or near clouds, altitude, aircraft type and duration. |
| [[7-1-22|AIM 7-1-22 — Wind Shear PIREPs]] | Report wind shear as the airspeed loss or gain and the altitudes where it happened ('loss of 20 knots at 400'), never as 'negative' or 'positive' wind shear, which has been misread as no shear. Tell ATC when you fly a wind shear escape maneuver and again when it is complete. |
| [[7-1-23|AIM 7-1-23 — Clear Air Turbulence (CAT) PIREPs]] | Clear air turbulence is a serious hazard mostly above 15,000 ft; the only source of information is pilots, so report the time, location and intensity of any CAT encounter. |
| [[7-1-24|AIM 7-1-24 — Microbursts]] | Microbursts are intense small-scale downdrafts (up to 6,000 fpm) that spread out on reaching the ground, producing horizontal winds of 45 knots and a 90-knot headwind-to-tailwind shear across a diameter of under a mile (outflow about 2.5 miles). They intensify for about 5 minutes after touching down and seldom last more than 15. They occur in heavy rain and in benign-looking virga, where a ring of blowing dust may be the only clue. Airports with LLWAS, TDWR or WSP issue alerts such as 'microburst alert, 35 knot loss 2 mile final'. |
| [[7-1-26|AIM 7-1-26 — Thunderstorms]] | Thunderstorms carry turbulence, hail, icing, lightning and up- and downdrafts, and the visible cloud is only part of the system: severe turbulence extends up to 20 miles from a severe storm and about 10 from a less severe one. Radar shows precipitation, not turbulence. Lightning strikes are most likely between -5 and +5 degrees C, even in clear air near the storm. A METAR has no 'severe' flag, but 50-knot winds or 3/4-inch hail in the report mean a severe storm. |
| [[7-1-27|AIM 7-1-27 — Thunderstorm Flying]] | Thunderstorm do's and don'ts: never take off or land facing an approaching storm, never fly under one or under its anvil, never trust its appearance, never assume ATC will vector you around it, and never use data-linked NEXRAD (15 to 20 minutes old) for tactical maneuvering. Do avoid severe storms by 20 miles, circumnavigate an area with 6/10 coverage, treat tops of 35,000 ft or more as extremely hazardous, give a PIREP, and divert and wait it out if you cannot get around. If penetration is unavoidable: belts tight, hold course and a constant attitude, pick an altitude below the freezing level or above -15 C, pitot heat on, penetration speed set, lights up, autopilot altitude and speed hold off, and never turn back once inside. |
| [[7-1-28|AIM 7-1-28 — Key to Aerodrome Forecast (TAF) and Aviation Routine Weather Report (METAR)]] | The key to decoding TAFs and METARs: message type, station, issuance time in UTC, TAF valid period of 24 or 30 hours, wind as true direction and speed in knots with gusts and variability, visibility in statute miles (P6SM means more than six), runway visual range, present weather with intensity (- light, none moderate, + heavy) and proximity (VC means 5 to 10 miles away), sky cover in eighths (FEW 1 to 2, SCT 3 to 4, BKN 5 to 7, OVC 8) with heights in hundreds of feet, temperature and dew point in Celsius, altimeter in inches, and the TAF change groups FM, TEMPO, PROB30 and BECMG. |
| [[7-2-1|AIM 7-2-1 — General]] | Altimeters are subject to instrument error, static-system position error, nonstandard pressure and nonstandard temperature; the standard setting of 29.92 at high altitude removes the pressure-source errors between aircraft. |
| [[7-2-2|AIM 7-2-2 — Barometric Pressure Altimeter Errors]] | Extreme pressure: many altimeters cannot be set above 31.00 inches, and when the pressure is higher your true altitude is higher than indicated. Below 28.00 inches flight is not recommended if the altimeter cannot be set that low, because true altitude is then lower than indicated, and cold temperatures make it worse. |
| [[7-2-3|AIM 7-2-3 — Altimeter Errors]] | Altimeter procedures: on the ground, a reading 75 ft or more off field elevation makes the altimeter questionable. Flying from high pressure into low without resetting puts you closer to the ground than indicated, about 1,000 ft per inch of error. Below 18,000 ft set a station within 100 nm; above 31.00 inches a NOTAM has everyone set 31.00 en route, with no extra restrictions on VFR aircraft; at 18,000 ft and above set 29.92, and the lowest usable flight level rises as pressure falls (FL180 at 29.92 or higher, FL190 down to 28.92, FL200 below that). |
| [[7-4-1|AIM 7-4-1 — General]] | Every aircraft producing lift trails two counter-rotating vortices, and a wake encounter can impose rolling moments beyond the roll control of the following aircraft, injuring occupants and damaging the airframe. |
| [[7-4-5|AIM 7-4-5 — Operations Problem Areas]] | Where wake bites: below and behind the generating aircraft, especially at low altitude. Be alert in calm wind when vortices linger in the touchdown zone, drift from a nearby runway, sink into a crossing runway's path, into the traffic pattern, or into the VFR hemispheric altitude 500 ft below an airliner; a visual approach behind landing traffic is a common scenario. Visualize the vortex trail and stay above it. |
| [[7-4-6|AIM 7-4-6 — Vortex Avoidance Procedures]] | The avoidance recipes. Landing behind a larger aircraft on the same runway: stay at or above its approach path and land beyond its touchdown point; with a parallel runway closer than 2,500 ft, expect drift; on a crossing runway, cross above its path. Landing behind a departing large aircraft: land well before its rotation point. Departing behind one: rotate before its rotation point, climb above its path and turn away upwind. Be wary after a large aircraft's low approach or touch-and-go, and en route stay out from below and behind, moving laterally upwind. |
| [[7-4-8|AIM 7-4-8 — Pilot Responsibility]] | Accepting traffic information, an instruction to follow an aircraft, or a visual approach clearance means you accept responsibility for wake separation. ATC says 'super' or 'heavy' when known. Lighter aircraft on a visual approach behind them should fly on or above the glidepath (the 3-to-1 rule: 3,000 ft at 10 miles), pick a landing point at least 1,000 ft down the runway and beyond the preceding aircraft's touchdown, keep the line of sight to it above and ahead of the heavier aircraft, ask ATC for separation updates, and report any wake encounter. |
| [[7-6-7|AIM 7-6-7 — Mountain Flying]] | Mountain flying: file a flight plan and route over populated areas and known passes with altitude to glide clear; do not fly a light aircraft when winds aloft exceed 35 mph; expect downdrafts of 1,500 to 2,000 fpm on the lee side and add a thousand feet approaching from downwind; cross ridges at a 45-degree angle so you can turn away. Mountain obscuration is its own hazard. At high-elevation fields use the same indicated airspeed but expect a faster true airspeed and longer landing; performance charts assume 59 F and 29.92, and towers at airports of 2,000 ft or higher broadcast 'check density altitude' on hot days. |
| [[7-7-2|AIM 7-7-2 — Aircraft Accident and Incident Reporting]] | NTSB notification: the operator must immediately notify the nearest NTSB office of an accident or of these incidents: flight control malfunction, a required crewmember unable to perform, turbine engine structural failure, in-flight fire, mid-air collision, property damage over \$25,000, and an overdue aircraft believed in an accident (plus electrical, hydraulic and engine cases for large aircraft). A report on NTSB Form 6120.1 is due within 10 days of an accident or when an overdue aircraft is still missing after 7 days; an incident report only if the NTSB asks. |
| [[8-1-1|AIM 8-1-1 — Fitness For Flight]] | Fitness for flight: a medical certificate, and the § 61.53 rule that a known disqualifying condition grounds you even with one. Do not fly with any illness; do not fly on medication unless the FAA approves it; alcohol impairs with as little as one drink and is detectable for at least 3 hours, the rule is 8 hours bottle to throttle and you may still be impaired after that; fatigue (acute and chronic), sleep apnea, stress and emotional upset each degrade judgment. The FAA's personal checklist: I'M SAFE, not impaired by Illness, Medication, Stress, Alcohol, Fatigue, Emotion. |
| [[8-1-2|AIM 8-1-2 — Effects of Altitude]] | Effects of altitude. Hypoxia: night vision degrades from 5,000 ft; significant effects usually start above 12,000 ft (impaired judgment, memory, coordination, euphoria); above 15,000 ft tunnel vision and cyanosis; the ability to act is lost in 20 to 30 minutes at 18,000 ft and 5 to 12 minutes at 20,000. Smoking, carbon monoxide, anemia and some medications lower the threshold. Use oxygen above 10,000 ft by day and 5,000 ft at night. Ear and sinus blocks come from flying with a cold and are not fixed by sprays. After scuba diving wait 12 hours (24 if a decompression stop was needed) before flying up to 8,000 ft and 24 hours before flying higher. |
| [[8-1-3|AIM 8-1-3 — Hyperventilation in Flight]] | Hyperventilation, rapid deep breathing under stress, blows off carbon dioxide and causes lightheadedness, tingling and a feeling of suffocation that can spiral. It clears in minutes when breathing is consciously slowed, or by breathing into a paper bag. Because early symptoms mimic hypoxia and both can occur together, if you are on oxygen go to 100 percent first and check the system before working on your breathing. |
| [[8-1-4|AIM 8-1-4 — Carbon Monoxide Poisoning in Flight]] | Carbon monoxide is colorless, odorless and tasteless and comes from exhaust; cabin heaters that use air passed over the exhaust manifold leak it through cracks. If you smell exhaust or get a headache, drowsiness or dizziness with the heater on, suspect CO: shut off the heater, open the vents, and get medical help if symptoms persist. |
| [[8-1-5|AIM 8-1-5 — Illusions in Flight]] | Illusions leading to spatial disorientation: the leans, Coriolis, graveyard spin and spiral, somatogravic (acceleration feels like nose-up), inversion, elevator, false horizon and autokinesis; they are prevented only by reference to fixed ground points or the instruments. Illusions leading to landing errors: a narrow or upsloping runway, featureless terrain and rain or haze all make you feel high and fly a low approach; a wide or downsloping runway does the opposite; fog feels like pitching up; bright lights make the runway seem closer. |
| [[8-1-6|AIM 8-1-6 — Vision in Flight]] | Vision: full dark adaptation takes 30 minutes, moderate adaptation 20 minutes under dim red light, and red light distorts chart colors; sunglasses should absorb 85 percent of light equally across colors. Scanning: only the fovea sees sharply, so scan in sectors, spend no more than a quarter to a third of the time inside (4 to 5 seconds on the panel per 16 outside), allow seconds to refocus between panel and horizon, and beware empty-field myopia above clouds or in haze, where the eyes relax to 10 to 30 ft. |
| [[8-1-8|AIM 8-1-8 — Judgment Aspects of Collision Avoidance]] | Collision-avoidance judgment: use the horizon to judge relative altitude (above the horizon means higher), know the right-of-way rules so you can act, expect the other pilot to maneuver too, and treat any target with no relative motion that grows in size as a collision course. High-hazard areas are airways near VORs and surface areas; most collisions happen in good weather; keep the windshield clean, cockpit organized, lights on, and use ATC advisories. |

| [[9-1-5|AIM 9-1-5 — General Description of Each Chart Series]] | The chart series a VFR pilot uses: sectionals (1:500,000, for visual navigation of slow to medium speed aircraft, with topography, landmarks, airspace and airports), terminal area charts (larger scale around Class B, with VFR flyway planning charts on the back), the Grand Canyon and Gulf Coast specials, and the Chart Supplement (airport, NAVAID, frequency and weather-source data in seven volumes, revised every 56 days) plus airport diagrams. IFR en route and terminal procedure charts and the planning charts round out the catalog. |
